At the end of the previous chapter, we confronted the fundamental limitation of the travelling-wave amplifier: despite containing massive amounts of stored atomic energy via population inversion, it is ultimately just a powerful optical megaphone waiting for a voice. It strictly requires an external seed signal to trigger stimulated emission.

In this chapter, we eliminate the need for an external input entirely. By taking that exact same active gain medium and trapping it inside a resonant optical cavity---between two highly reflective mirrors---we introduce \textbf{optical feedback}. A few initially random, spontaneously emitted photons will now become trapped and bounce back and forth, triggering a massive avalanche of stimulated emission on every pass. By closing the loop, we have transformed our open-loop amplifier into a self-sustained \textbf{laser oscillator} (Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation).

    \centering

    \caption{From Amplifier to Oscillator: A single-pass Travelling-Wave Amplifier requires an external seed signal and provides no feedback. Adding two mirrors to form a Fabry-P\'{e}rot cavity traps the light inside the gain medium, enabling self-sustained oscillation without any external input.}
\end{figure}

Our primary goal in this chapter is to understand the exact mechanics of this transformation. We must map how trapping an active gain medium inside a geometrical cavity dictates the fundamental oscillation conditions, the allowed mode frequencies, the threshold pump rate, and the final output power of a running laser.

\section{The Mechanics of the Laser Oscillator}

\subsection{The Three Conditions for Oscillation}

A laser is, at its core, an oscillator in the strict engineering sense. Just like any electronic or mechanical oscillator, it requires exactly three functional components to successfully generate a stable, self-sustaining signal. In a laser, each of these abstract requirements maps directly to a physical optical mechanism we have already derived:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A Noise Source (Spontaneous Emission):} Every oscillator needs an initial seed to spark the process. In a laser, this seed is provided by random spontaneous emission from the excited atoms. While these photons are initially fired in random directions and phases, the tiny fraction that happen to emit directly along the longitudinal cavity axis get amplified and eventually dominate the entire intracavity field.

    \item \textbf{A Selective Feedback Mechanism (The Fabry-P\'{e}rot Cavity):} Feedback routes the optical output directly back to the input. We achieve this by placing two mirrors facing each other across the gain medium, forcing photons to bounce back and forth and accumulate exponential gain on every pass. This mirror assembly also acts as an aggressive frequency filter: only the discrete resonant modes of the cavity experience constructive round-trip interference and survive.

    \item \textbf{A Stabilising Nonlinearity (Gain Saturation):} If the round-trip gain stayed mathematically above unity forever, the intracavity power would grow toward infinity. To prevent this, the system relies on the saturation physics we derived previously:
          \begin{equation*}
              \gamma = \frac{\gamma_0}{1 + \Phi/\Phi_s}
          \end{equation*}
          As the photon flux $\Phi$ builds up inside the cavity, it rapidly depletes the population inversion, forcing the overall gain coefficient $\gamma$ downward. The oscillation ultimately stabilises at a finite, perfectly balanced steady-state power where the saturated intracavity gain exactly equals the physical cavity losses.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The Fabry-P\'{e}rot Cavity --- Round-Trip Analysis}

Now let's talk about the heart of the laser: the Fabry-P\'{e}rot cavity. This cavity consists of a gain medium of length $L$ between two mirrors $M_1$ and $M_2$ with field reflectivities $r_1$ and $r_2$ (and power reflectivities $R_1 = r_1^2$ and $R_2 = r_2^2$). The gain medium has gain coefficient $\gamma$ and internal loss coefficient $\alpha_s$ (which accounts for scattering and non-resonant absorption, since resonant absorption by the lasing atoms is inherently handled by the net gain coefficient $\gamma$).

We start by asking what happens to the electric field amplitude as it completes one full round trip. Let the field amplitude at a reference point just inside mirror $M_1$ be $E_1$.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{Figures/Chapter 4/fp_cavity_round_trip.jpg}
    \caption{Fabry-P\'{e}rot Cavity Round Trip: The electric field amplitude at each stage of one complete round trip, from $E_1$ (reference) through forward propagation $E_2$, reflection $E_3$, backward propagation $E_4$, and final reflection $E_5$.}
\end{figure}

The field propagates from $M_1$ to $M_2$ over length $L$, accumulating a phase factor $e^{-j\beta L}$ and an amplitude factor $e^{-\alpha_s L/2}\,e^{\gamma L/2}$ (one-half exponents convert from power to field amplitude):
\begin{equation*}
    E_2 = E_1\,e^{-j\beta L}\,e^{(\gamma - \alpha_s)L/2}
\end{equation*}

The field then reflects off $M_2$ with field reflectivity $r_2$:
\begin{equation*}
    E_3 = r_2\,E_1\,e^{-j\beta L}\,e^{(\gamma - \alpha_s)L/2}
\end{equation*}

It propagates back from $M_2$ to $M_1$, picking up the same exponential factors again:
\begin{equation*}
    E_4 = r_2\,E_1\,e^{-j2\beta L}\,e^{(\gamma - \alpha_s)L}
\end{equation*}

Finally, it reflects off $M_1$ with field reflectivity $r_1$:
\begin{equation*}
    E_5 = r_1 r_2\,E_1\,e^{-j2\beta L}\,e^{(\gamma - \alpha_s)L}
\end{equation*}

After one complete round trip, the returned field $E_5$ is related to the starting field $E_1$ by:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{E_5}{E_1} = r_1 r_2\,e^{-j2\beta L}\,e^{(\gamma - \alpha_s)L}
\end{equation*}

Sustained oscillation requires $E_5 = E_1$ --- the field must exactly reproduce itself each round trip. This single vector equation splits into separate phase and amplitude conditions.

\subsubsection{The phase condition --- longitudinal modes}

The phase component of the oscillation condition requires $e^{-j2\beta L} = 1$, meaning the round-trip phase must be an integer multiple of $2\pi$:
\begin{equation*}
    2\beta L = 2q\pi, \qquad q = 1,\,2,\,3,\,\ldots
\end{equation*}

The propagation constant in a medium is $\beta = 2\pi\nu/c$. Substituting this:
\begin{equation*}
    2\cdot\frac{2\pi\nu}{c}\cdot L = 2q\pi
\end{equation*}
Solving for $\nu$, and denoting the discrete allowed frequencies as $\nu_q$:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        \nu_q = q\cdot\frac{c}{2L} = q\,\nu_F, \qquad q = 1,\,2,\,3,\,\ldots
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\nu_q$: the frequency of the $q$-th \textbf{longitudinal mode} of the cavity.
    \item $\nu_F = \frac{c}{2L}$: the \textbf{Free Spectral Range} (FSR), representing the frequency spacing between adjacent modes.
    \item $q$: the longitudinal mode number (an integer; in practice $q \sim 10^5$--$10^6$ for optical cavities).
    \item $c$: the speed of light in the medium.
\end{itemize}

Only these discrete frequencies satisfy the phase condition. At any other frequency, successive round trips interfere destructively and the field dies. These $\nu_q$ are called the \textbf{cold-resonator modes} because they depend only on the cavity geometry --- not on the gain medium. The phase condition is purely a geometric constraint: the round-trip path length $2L$ must contain exactly $q$ whole wavelengths in the medium.

\subsubsection{The amplitude condition --- threshold gain $\alpha_r$}

To sustain or build up oscillation, the field amplitude must not decay after a round trip. Therefore, the amplitude part of the round-trip condition requires $|E_5| \geq |E_1|$, which means:
\begin{equation*}
    \left|r_1 r_2\right|\cdot e^{(\gamma - \alpha_s)L} \geq 1 \quad \implies \quad \ln(|r_1 r_2|) + (\gamma - \alpha_s)L \geq 0
\end{equation*}

Since the field reflectivity magnitudes are related to power reflectivities by $|r_i| = \sqrt{R_i}$, we have $\ln(|r_1 r_2|) = \tfrac{1}{2}\ln(R_1 R_2)$. Rearranging for $\gamma$:
\begin{equation*}
    \gamma L \geq \alpha_s L - \tfrac{1}{2}\ln(R_1 R_2) \quad\implies\quad \gamma L \geq \alpha_s L + \tfrac{1}{2}\ln\!\left(\frac{1}{R_1 R_2}\right) \quad\implies\quad     \gamma \geq \alpha_s + \frac{1}{2L}\ln\!\left(\frac{1}{R_1 R_2}\right)
\end{equation*}

The right-hand side is a fixed geometric constant. We define this exact boundary value as the \textbf{threshold gain} ($\gamma_{\text{th}}$), which is equal to the \textbf{total loss coefficient} ($\alpha_r$):
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        \gamma_{\text{th}} = \alpha_s + \frac{1}{2L}\ln\!\left(\frac{1}{R_1 R_2}\right) \equiv \alpha_r
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\alpha_r$: the \textbf{total loss coefficient} --- the gain per unit length that must be achieved to sustain oscillation.
    \item $\alpha_s$: the internal scattering and \textit{non-resonant} absorption loss per unit length in the cavity.
    \item $\frac{1}{2L}\ln(1/R_1 R_2)$: the effective distributed loss per unit length due to useful power leaking out through the mirrors --- the \textbf{output coupling loss}.
\end{itemize}

The amplitude condition states that the gain coefficient must meet or exceed $\alpha_r$ to sustain oscillation. If $\gamma < \alpha_r$, the field decays after each round trip; if $\gamma > \alpha_r$, the field grows.

\subsection{The Turn-On Sequence and Gain Clamping}

In a Travelling Wave Amplifier (TWA), we pump the medium to create population inversion and then \textit{manually inject} an external optical signal for it to amplify. A laser oscillator, however, requires no external input. It starts from absolute nothingness through a strict, self-regulating sequence:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The Pump (Building Inversion):} When the laser is first turned on, provided the pump is strong enough, it rapidly throws atoms into the excited state, raising the small-signal gain well above the cavity loss ($\gamma_0 > \alpha_r$). We now have a highly explosive, gain-heavy medium, but still no light.

    \item \textbf{The Spark (Spontaneous Emission):} Excited atoms don't stay excited forever. While waiting, some randomly drop back to the ground state and release photons in random directions (spontaneous emission). Most of these hit the cavity walls and are lost.

    \item \textbf{The Avalanche (Stimulated Emission):} By pure statistical chance, a few spontaneous photons are emitted exactly along the horizontal axis of the cavity, hitting the mirrors and becoming trapped. Because the gain strictly exceeds the total cavity loss ($\gamma > \alpha_r$), every round trip creates more new photons via stimulated emission than are lost through the mirrors. As these trapped photons bounce back and forth, their numbers don't just survive---they trigger a massive net avalanche. One photon creates two, two become four, four become eight. The number of photons bouncing between the mirrors---the \textbf{intracavity photon flux}---grows exponentially.

    \item \textbf{Gain Clamping (Steady State):} The avalanche does not grow to infinity. As the internal light population explodes, it ``eats'' the excited atoms (via stimulated emission) at an incredibly massive rate. This depletion physically forces the operating gain $\gamma$ to drop (a process called \textbf{gain saturation}). The field continues to grow---and the gain continues to drop---until the gain falls to exactly match the cavity loss ($\gamma = \alpha_r$).
\end{enumerate}

At this equilibrium, the intense light depletes the population inversion exactly as fast as the pump supplies it. Round-trip amplification now perfectly offsets round-trip loss, driving the system to a steady state. This creates a strict negative feedback loop: if the gain rises above $\alpha_r$, stimulated emission increases and rapidly depletes the inversion back down. If the gain drops below $\alpha_r$, photons escape the cavity faster than they are created, allowing the pump to rebuild the inversion. Consequently, the steady-state gain is strictly clamped to $\alpha_r$.

\subsection{Types of Mirrors}

Since the mirrors appear explicitly in the threshold gain condition through $R_1 R_2$, it is worth pausing to understand what kinds of mirrors are used in practice.

\subsubsection{Simple discontinuity}

The simplest mirror is an abrupt interface between two media with different refractive indices. At a planar boundary between media with indices $n_1$ and $n_2$, the Fresnel formula gives the field reflectivity at normal incidence:
\begin{equation*}
    r = \frac{n_2 - n_1}{n_2 + n_1}
\end{equation*}

For an interface between an active gain medium (with refractive index $n$) and air ($n \approx 1$):
\begin{equation*}
    r = \frac{n - 1}{n + 1}, \qquad R = \left(\frac{n-1}{n+1}\right)^{\!2}
\end{equation*}

Depending on the refractive index of the medium, this bare reflection alone can sometimes be large enough to sustain laser oscillation. For instance, a high-index medium with $n \approx 3.5$ yields $R \approx 31\%$, which is often sufficient to act as a mirror without requiring any special reflective coating. The cleanly cleaved ends of the host crystal itself simply act as the cavity mirrors.

\subsubsection{Free-standing dielectric mirror}

A more versatile option is a free-standing dielectric mirror --- a thin slab or multilayer stack of dielectric material placed in the cavity. By choosing the thicknesses and refractive indices appropriately, almost any desired reflectivity $R(\lambda)$ can be engineered over a chosen wavelength range. This gives the laser designer fine control over both the output coupling loss and the frequency selectivity of the cavity.

\section{Laser Thresholds and Inversion}

We now know what $\alpha_r$ is and what the gain must equal for oscillation to begin ($\gamma_0 \geq \alpha_r$). But this abstract condition doesn't tell us how to actually build the laser. We need to translate this into physical parameters we control: how large must the population inversion be, and how hard must we pump the system to achieve it?

\subsection{Threshold Population Inversion}

The threshold population inversion ($N_{0,\text{th}}$) is the critical difference in atomic population ($N_2 - N_1$) required to generate exactly enough stimulated emission to balance the total cavity loss.

The gain coefficient is directly proportional to the population inversion $N_0$ and the stimulated emission cross section $\sigma$:
\begin{equation*}
    \gamma_0 = \sigma\cdot N_0
\end{equation*}

At the lasing threshold, the small-signal gain must exactly equal the total cavity loss:
\begin{equation*}
    \gamma_{\text{th}} = \alpha_r \quad\Rightarrow\quad \sigma\,N_{0,\text{th}} = \alpha_r
\end{equation*}

Solving for the \textbf{threshold population inversion}:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        N_{0,\text{th}} = \frac{\alpha_r}{\sigma}
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

While the threshold condition $N_{0,\text{th}} = \alpha_r/\sigma$ is a general requirement, laser oscillation will naturally begin at the frequency that requires the minimum pump power. Because the required inversion is inversely proportional to the emission cross-section ($N_{0,\text{th}} \propto 1/\sigma$), this minimum occurs where the cross-section is maximized---at the atomic line centre, $\nu_0$.

In practice, the discrete resonant modes of the cavity rarely align perfectly with the exact atomic peak. However, because cavity modes are typically densely spaced relative to the atomic linewidth ($\text{FSR} \ll \Delta\nu$), there will inevitably be a mode close enough to $\nu_0$ that we can treat them as perfectly aligned. Evaluating the cross-section at this peak for a Lorentzian lineshape ($g(\nu_0) = 2/(\pi\Delta\nu)$) yields:
\begin{equation*}
    \sigma\big|_{\nu=\nu_0} = \frac{\lambda^2}{8\pi\tau_{\text{sp}}}\cdot g_{\nu_0}(\nu_0)
    = \frac{\lambda^2}{8\pi\tau_{\text{sp}}}\cdot\frac{2}{\pi\,\Delta\nu}
    = \frac{\lambda^2}{4\pi^2\,\Delta\nu\,\tau_{\text{sp}}}
\end{equation*}

Substituting into the threshold inversion:
\begin{equation*}
    N_{0,\text{th}} = \alpha_r\cdot\frac{4\pi^2\,\Delta\nu\,\tau_{\text{sp}}}{\lambda^2}
\end{equation*}

The threshold inversion can be rewritten in a very illuminating way by introducing the \textbf{photon lifetime} ($\tau_{\text{ph}}$). Physically, this is simply the average time a photon survives bouncing around inside the cavity before it either escapes through the mirrors or is absorbed by internal scattering. Mathematically, it is the inverse of the cavity's temporal decay rate, given by $\tau_{\text{ph}} = 1/(V_g \alpha_r)$ (which we will rigorously derive in the Resonator section). Taking $V_g \approx c$ in the medium, we substitute $\alpha_r = 1/(c\,\tau_{\text{ph}})$ to get:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        N_{0,\text{th}} = \frac{4\pi^2\,\Delta\nu\,\tau_{\text{sp}}}{\lambda^2\,c\,\tau_{\text{ph}}}
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

The physical message is transparent: a shorter photon lifetime (lossier cavity), a larger atomic linewidth, or a shorter in-medium wavelength all require a higher threshold inversion before lasing can begin.

\subsection{Threshold Pump Rate}

We can use the rate equations to determine the threshold pump rate. For example, in the case of a three-level laser at steady state with no intracavity signal present, the small-signal population inversion established by a pump rate $R$ is given by:
\begin{equation*}
    N_0 = 2R\,\tau_{21} - N_a
\end{equation*}
By setting this inversion equal to the required threshold inversion, $N_0 = N_{0,\text{th}}$, we can solve directly for the threshold pump rate, $R_{\text{th}}$:
\begin{equation*}
    2R_{\text{th}}\,\tau_{21} - N_a = N_{0,\text{th}} \quad\implies\quad 2R_{\text{th}}\,\tau_{21} - N_a = \frac{\alpha_r}{\sigma\big|_{\nu=\nu_0}}
\end{equation*}
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        R_{\text{th}} = \frac{N_a}{2\tau_{21}} + \frac{\alpha_r}{2\sigma\big|_{\nu=\nu_0}\,\tau_{21}}
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

The first term $N_a/(2\tau_{21})$ is the pump rate needed just to overcome the ground-state population and reach transparency ($N_2 = N_1$). The second term is the additional pumping required to push the inversion up to the level $N_{0,\text{th}}$ needed to overcome the cavity losses.

Without any pumping, $\gamma$ is negative (absorption); as $R$ increases, $\gamma$ rises; at $R = R_{\text{th}}$, the gain at line centre just reaches $\alpha_r$ and laser oscillation begins. Further pumping above threshold builds up a large intracavity photon flux.

Once the pump has crossed threshold, the next question is no longer whether lasing is possible, but \emph{which} of the allowed longitudinal modes survive after gain saturation sets in.

\section{Mode Selection at Turn-On}

Now that we understand the conditions for lasing, we need to ask which frequencies actually lase. The phase condition allows only the discrete frequencies $\nu_q = q\,\nu_F$, but not all of these have sufficient gain to overcome the loss. The interplay between the gain profile shape and the loss level determines which modes oscillate at steady state.

The condition for a mode at frequency $\nu_q$ to start oscillating is simply:
\begin{equation*}
    \gamma\big|_{\nu=\nu_q} > \alpha_r
\end{equation*}

All modes whose frequencies fall within the band where the small-signal gain exceeds the loss will initially begin to grow. The number of such modes is approximately:
\begin{equation*}
    \text{Number of lasing modes} \approx \frac{B}{\nu_F}
\end{equation*}
where $B$ is the bandwidth over which $\gamma_0 > \alpha_r$ and $\nu_F = c/2L$ is the FSR.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{Figures/Chapter 4/available_gain_bandwidth_modes.jpg}

Only discrete FSR modes (the vertical comb) that fall within $B$ satisfy $\gamma\big|_{\nu=\nu_q} > \alpha_r$ and are able to oscillate. Modes outside $B$ are immediately suppressed.}
\end{figure}

The precise outcome at start-up also depends on how the atomic line centre $\nu_0$ happens to align with the discrete mode comb --- a purely geometric consideration independent of the broadening mechanism. Three distinct scenarios arise naturally:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Line centre coincides exactly with a longitudinal mode} ($\nu_0 = \nu_q$ for some integer $q$): This mode enjoys the maximum small-signal gain and grows fastest from the very first round trip. Provided this peak gain exceeds $\alpha_r$, it becomes the natural dominant mode. Whether nearby modes survive alongside it ultimately depends on whether the gain saturation is homogeneous or inhomogeneous.

    \item \textbf{Line centre falls between two adjacent modes}: No single mode sits exactly at the gain peak. The mode physically closest to $\nu_0$ has the highest gain among all candidates and begins growing fastest. The physical phenomenon of frequency pulling further drags this dominant mode's oscillation frequency toward $\nu_0$, reinforcing its advantage.

    \item \textbf{Two modes are placed symmetrically about the line centre} ($\nu_0$ sits exactly halfway between $\nu_q$ and $\nu_{q+1}$): Both modes see identical small-signal gains and grow at the exact same rate. This creates an initially degenerate system. Even under strict homogeneous broadening, these perfectly symmetric modes can theoretically co-lase and share available power. In practice, however, any microscopic mechanical or thermal cavity length drift will quickly break the exact symmetry, causing one mode to ultimately win.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Homogeneous Broadening: Single-Mode Lasing}

\subsection{Gain Saturation and Single-Mode Lasing}

In a homogeneously broadened medium, all active atoms are spectrally identical---they interact with the optical field uniformly across the entire frequency spectrum. Consequently, all cavity modes compete for the exact same pool of inverted atoms; if one mode depletes the inversion, the available gain drops for every other mode simultaneously.

As the dominant mode (at frequency $\nu_q$ near the line centre) grows, its increasing photon flux ($\Phi$) stimulates emission, depleting the global population inversion $N$. Because all atoms are spectrally identical, this single depleted $N$ dictates the gain for all frequencies simultaneously ($\gamma = \sigma N$). The saturated gain curve is therefore scaled down by a constant factor determined exclusively by the lasing mode: $\gamma = \frac{\gamma_0}{1 + \Phi/\Phi_s}$, where the saturation flux $\Phi_s$ is evaluated strictly at the lasing frequency $\nu_q$.

Because the \emph{entire} gain profile is suppressed globally by this exact same scalar factor, a "winner-takes-all" dynamic occurs. The dominant mode grows until its saturated gain exactly clamps to the round-trip cavity loss ($\gamma = \alpha_r$). Since the entire curve is pulled down together, the gain at all other mode frequencies is forced strictly below the loss threshold ($\gamma < \alpha_r$). These competing modes can no longer sustain oscillation and die out. \textbf{The ultimate result of homogeneous broadening is therefore strictly single-mode lasing.}

At steady state the gain touches $\alpha_r$ only at the winning mode frequency, driving the gain at all other mode frequencies below $\alpha_r$ simultaneously. All competing modes die.}

\subsection{Steady-State Intracavity Photon Flux}

At steady state, gain saturation has driven the gain exactly to the loss level: $\gamma = \alpha_r$. For a homogeneously broadened medium:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\gamma_0}{1 + \Phi/\Phi_s} = \alpha_r
\end{equation*}

Solving for the intracavity photon-flux density $\Phi$:
\begin{equation*}
    1 + \frac{\Phi}{\Phi_s} = \frac{\gamma_0}{\alpha_r}
    \quad\Rightarrow\quad
    \frac{\Phi}{\Phi_s} = \frac{\gamma_0}{\alpha_r} - 1
\end{equation*}

\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        \Phi = \Phi_s \left( \frac{\gamma_0}{\alpha_r} - 1 \right)
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

This explicitly defines the threshold behaviour: steady-state oscillation occurs only when the small-signal gain exceeds the cavity losses ($\gamma_0 > \alpha_r$). Below this threshold, the laser cannot oscillate, and the flux remains zero. Note that this intracavity photon flux, $\Phi$, accounts for the total flux of photons travelling in both directions within the cavity.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{Figures/Chapter 4/steady_state_inversion_and_flux.jpg}
    \caption{Gain Clamping at Threshold: Below threshold the steady-state inversion $N$ rises linearly with pump rate while the intracavity flux remains zero. Once the pump crosses threshold ($N_0 = N_\mathrm{th}$), oscillation begins and inversion clamps to exactly $N_\mathrm{th}$ regardless of further pumping. The excess pump energy instead drives linear growth in the steady-state intracavity photon flux $\Phi$ (right axis).}

\begin{misconception}[Is Gain Clamping a Waste of Excess Pump Power?]
    \textbf{The Misconception:} If the population inversion $N$ clamps strictly at $N_{\text{th}}$ regardless of how hard we pump, the excess pump power above threshold must simply be wasted as heat or spontaneous emission.

    \textbf{The Physical Reality:} It is exactly the opposite! Because $N$ is clamped, the rate of spontaneous emission (which is proportional to $N$) is "frozen" at its threshold value and cannot increase further.

    Therefore, \textbf{100\% of the excess pump power is forced into stimulated emission}. Gain clamping is the exact mechanism that caps wasteful losses and strictly channels all additional input energy into the coherent laser beam.
\end{misconception}

\subsection{Output Power and Optimal Mirror Coupling}

To model a practical laser, we assume a \textbf{single output mirror} configuration: the back mirror is a perfect reflector ($R_1 = 1$), and the front mirror is the output coupler ($R_2 = R$).

Although the cavity is asymmetric, practical lasers operate with high-reflectivity mirrors ($R \approx 1$). Because very little light escapes per pass, the intensities of the forward-travelling and backward-travelling waves remain almost identical everywhere inside the cavity. Under this high-Q approximation, the total intracavity photon flux $\Phi$ is distributed equally between the two directions.

The physical output power exiting the laser is exactly the energy carried by the forward-travelling half of the flux passing through the beam area $A$ and transmitting through $M_2$ (with power transmission $T = 1 - R$):
\begin{equation*}
    P_{\text{out}} = \left(\frac{\Phi}{2}\right) h\nu\cdot A\cdot T
\end{equation*}

Substituting our derived steady-state flux $\Phi = \Phi_s(\gamma_0/\alpha_r - 1)$:
\begin{equation*}
    P_{\text{out}} = \frac{1}{2} \left[ \Phi_s \left( \frac{\gamma_0}{\alpha_r} - 1 \right) \right] h\nu\cdot A\cdot T \quad \Rightarrow \quad P_{\text{out}} = \frac{\Phi_s\,h\nu\,A}{2}\cdot T\left(\frac{\gamma_0}{\alpha_r} - 1\right)
\end{equation*}

By defining the macroscopic \textbf{saturation power} $P_s = \Phi_s\,h\nu\,A$, we encapsulate the medium's intrinsic saturation properties and beam geometry into a single power term, simplifying the expression to:
\begin{equation*}
    P_{\text{out}} = \frac{P_s}{2}\cdot T\left(\frac{\gamma_0}{\alpha_r} - 1\right)
\end{equation*}

The total cavity loss coefficient, $\alpha_r$, is the sum of the internal scattering and absorption loss, $\alpha_s$, and the distributed mirror loss. For highly reflective mirrors ($R \approx 1$), the transmissivity $T = 1 - R$ is sufficiently small ($T \ll 1$) to justify the Taylor approximation, $\ln(1-T) \approx -T$. By substituting this into the mirror loss term, the algebraic steps gracefully simplify:
\begin{equation*}
    \alpha_r = \alpha_s + \frac{1}{2L}\ln\left(\frac{1}{R}\right) \quad \Rightarrow \quad \alpha_r = \alpha_s - \frac{1}{2L}\ln(1-T) \quad \Rightarrow \quad \alpha_r \approx \alpha_s - \frac{1}{2L}(-T) = \alpha_s + \frac{T}{2L}
\end{equation*}

Next, substitute this explicit loss directly back into our power equation. To eliminate the per-length dependence inside the bracket, we multiply the numerator and denominator of the gain-to-loss ratio by the round-trip path length $2L$:
\begin{equation*}
    P_{\text{out}} = \frac{P_s}{2}\cdot T\left( \frac{\gamma_0}{\alpha_s + \dfrac{T}{2L}} - 1 \right) \quad \Rightarrow \quad P_{\text{out}} = \frac{P_s}{2}\cdot T\left( \frac{2\gamma_0 L}{2\alpha_s L + T} - 1 \right)
\end{equation*}
We now recognise the standard unitless macroscopic round-trip variables: the round-trip small-signal gain $g = 2\gamma_0 L$ and the round-trip internal loss $\ell = 2\alpha_s L$. Substituting these definitions transforms the equation into a purely macroscopic form:
\begin{equation*}
    P_{\text{out}} = \frac{P_s}{2}\cdot T\left( \frac{g}{\ell + T} - 1 \right)
\end{equation*}
Distributing the transmissivity $T$ explicitly separates the extracted power from the mirror loss, yielding the standard expression for the laser output power:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        P_{\text{out}} = \frac{P_s}{2}\left[ \frac{gT}{\ell + T} - T \right]
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

The structure of this equation reveals a fundamental physical tension governed by the mirror transmissivity $T = 1 - R$. The output power $P_{\text{out}} \propto \Phi \cdot T$ is the product of the steady-state intracavity flux $\Phi$ and the escape fraction $T$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Under-coupling (Small $T$):} Increasing the reflectivity ($R \to 1$) makes $T$ very small. This minimizes the total cavity loss ($\alpha_r = \alpha_s + \frac{T}{2L}$), allowing an enormous intracavity flux $\Phi = \Phi_s\left(\frac{\gamma_0}{\alpha_r} - 1\right)$ to build up. However, because the escape fraction $T$ is tiny, the actual output power is choked off. The intense light remains trapped inside and is wasted on the internal scattering/absorption loss $\alpha_s$.
    \item \textbf{Over-coupling (Large $T$):} Conversely, increasing $T$ to let more light escape sharply increases the cavity loss $\alpha_r$. This raises the lasing threshold and heavily depletes the intracavity flux $\Phi$. If $T$ is increased so much that the total loss approaches the small-signal gain ($\alpha_r \to \gamma_0$), the oscillation ceases entirely ($\Phi \to 0$) and the output power again collapses.
\end{itemize}
An optimal transmissivity $T_{\text{opt}}$ must therefore exist to perfectly balance building a strong internal field against allowing a large fraction of it to escape.

To find this optimum, we differentiate the output power with respect to $T$ and set the derivative to zero.
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{dP_{\text{out}}}{dT} = \frac{P_s}{2} \left[ \frac{g(\ell + T) - (gT)(1)}{(\ell + T)^2} - 1 \right] \quad \Rightarrow \quad \frac{dP_{\text{out}}}{dT} = \frac{P_s}{2} \left[ \frac{g\ell}{(\ell + T)^2} - 1 \right] = 0
\end{equation*}

Setting the bracket to zero isolates the condition for optimal transmissivity:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{g\ell}{(\ell + T_{\text{opt}})^2} = 1 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \ell + T_{\text{opt}} = \sqrt{g\ell}
\end{equation*}
Subtracting the internal loss $\ell$ yields the final expression for $T_{\text{opt}}$:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        T_{\text{opt}} = \sqrt{g\ell} - \ell
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

To find the absolute maximum power achievable by the laser, we substitute $T_{\text{opt}} = \sqrt{g\ell} - \ell$ directly into the standard power equation:
\begin{equation*}
    P_{\text{max}} = \frac{P_s}{2}\left[ \frac{g(\sqrt{g\ell} - \ell)}{\sqrt{g\ell}} - (\sqrt{g\ell} - \ell) \right] \quad \Rightarrow \quad P_{\text{max}} = \frac{P_s}{2} \left[ (g - \sqrt{g\ell}) - (\sqrt{g\ell} - \ell) \right]
\end{equation*}
Grouping the terms algebraically reveals a perfect quadratic expansion ($g - 2\sqrt{g\ell} + \ell$):
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        P_{\text{max}} = \frac{P_s}{2}\!\left(\sqrt{g} - \sqrt{\ell}\right)^{\!2}
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

    \caption{Optimal Mirror Transmissivity: Output power $P_\mathrm{out}$ as a function of mirror transmissivity $T$. In the under-coupling regime ($T < T_\mathrm{opt}$) most photons are trapped inside the cavity and little power exits. In the over-coupling regime ($T > T_\mathrm{opt}$) the round-trip loss exceeds the gain so severely that the intracavity power collapses. \section{Inhomogeneous Broadening: Multi-Mode Lasing}

\subsection{Gain Saturation in an Inhomogeneously Broadened Medium}

When we deal with an inhomogeneously broadened medium --- such as a gas laser where atoms are zipping around at varying velocities --- we can no longer treat all the active atoms as a single, identical pool. Instead, the total medium is fundamentally a collection of distinct sub-groups, or ``velocity classes'', where each group has its own Doppler-shifted resonance frequency. For a velocity class at velocity $V$, all atoms within it experience the exact same Doppler shift, so they behave as a perfectly \emph{homogeneously} broadened subgroup with a narrow Lorentzian lineshape centred at the shifted resonance frequency $\nu_0' = \nu_0(1 + V/c)$. The entire inhomogeneous gain profile is simply the statistical sum of all these individual homogeneous subgroups.

\subsubsection*{Saturation of a Single Velocity Class}

Consider a single velocity class moving at velocity $V$. Because all atoms in this subgroup share the exact same velocity, they all have their resonance peak shifted to the exact same frequency $\nu_0'$. They interact with the field identically and obey standard homogeneous saturation. Therefore, the saturated gain contribution $d\gamma$ from this infinitesimal slice of the population follows the standard homogeneous law:
\begin{equation*}
    d\gamma = \frac{d\gamma_0}{1 + \Phi / \Phi_s'}
\end{equation*}
where $d\gamma_0$ is the small-signal gain of this velocity class, and $\Phi_s'$ is the effective saturation flux required to saturate these specific atoms.

To understand $\Phi_s'$, we must look at the atoms' cross-section $\sigma$. Because every atom in the gas is identical, they all possess the exact same Lorentzian lineshape $g_{\nu_0'}(\nu) = \frac{\Delta\nu/2\pi}{(\Delta\nu/2)^2 + (\nu - \nu_0')^2}$. The atom's velocity simply shifts this curve left or right along the frequency axis.

If a velocity class is shifted such that its peak matches the laser frequency ($\nu = \nu_0'$), the laser hits it at its maximum possible cross-section. Because these resonant atoms interact so strongly with the light, they are the easiest to saturate. The flux required to saturate them is the absolute minimum possible, which we define as the intrinsic saturation photon flux density $\Phi_s$.

However, for most velocity classes, the laser frequency $\nu$ will miss their peak. As the laser hits the ``tail'' of their shifted Lorentzian, their effective cross-section drops proportionally to the normalized lineshape $\frac{g_{\nu_0'}(\nu)}{g_{\nu_0'}(\nu_0')}$. Because they interact weakly with the light, they are much harder to saturate, and their required saturation flux $\Phi_s'$ increases dramatically.

Since the required saturation flux $\Phi_s'$ is inversely proportional to the cross-section, its inverse scales directly with the normalized lineshape. This allows us to express the variable, velocity-dependent $\Phi_s'$ entirely in terms of the global constant $\Phi_s$. By explicitly expanding this ratio, which normalises the Lorentzian to a peak value of $1$, we can trace its algebraic simplification:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{1}{\Phi_s'} = \frac{1}{\Phi_s} \cdot \frac{g_{\nu_0'}(\nu)}{g_{\nu_0'}(\nu_0')} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{1}{\Phi_s'} = \frac{1}{\Phi_s} \cdot \frac{\dfrac{\Delta\nu/2\pi}{(\Delta\nu/2)^2 + (\nu - \nu_0')^2}}{\dfrac{\Delta\nu/2\pi}{(\Delta\nu/2)^2 + (\nu_0' - \nu_0')^2}} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{1}{\Phi_s'} = \frac{1}{\Phi_s} \cdot \frac{(\Delta\nu/2)^2}{(\Delta\nu/2)^2 + (\nu - \nu_0')^2}
\end{equation*}
By substituting this simplified relationship back into our gain equation, we create a universal saturation formula that works for any arbitrary velocity class, regardless of how badly detuned they are from the laser:
\begin{equation*}
    d\gamma = \frac{d\gamma_0}{1 + \dfrac{\Phi}{\Phi_s} \cdot \dfrac{(\Delta\nu/2)^2}{(\Delta\nu/2)^2 + (\nu - \nu_0')^2}}
\end{equation*}
This algebraic reduction confirms our physical intuition: velocity classes shifted far from resonance have near-zero interaction probability, rendering them virtually impossible to saturate. Crucially, while this apparent center frequency $\nu_0'$ varies for each velocity class, the intrinsic quantum mechanics of the atoms do not, meaning the saturation flux $\Phi_s$ remains a strict, global constant across the entire inhomogeneous medium.

\subsubsection*{Power Broadening and the Saturated Lineshape}

To see how the intracavity field distorts the gain profile of each velocity class, we must examine the frequency-dependent shape of the small-signal gain distribution. The absolute small-signal gain depends on both the transition cross-section and the population inversion density of that velocity class, governed by the exact relation $d\gamma_0 = \frac{\lambda^2}{8\pi\tau_{\mathrm{sp}}} g_{\nu_0'}(\nu) \, dN$, where $g_{\nu_0'}(\nu) = \frac{\Delta\nu/2\pi}{(\Delta\nu/2)^2 + (\nu - \nu_0')^2}$ is the unperturbed homogeneous Lorentzian lineshape of the velocity class.

Substituting this exact expression into our saturation equation, we can clear the nested fractions by multiplying the numerator and denominator by $(\Delta\nu/2)^2 + (\nu - \nu_0')^2$:
\begin{equation*}
    d\gamma = \frac{\frac{\lambda^2}{8\pi\tau_{\mathrm{sp}}} \left[ \frac{\frac{\Delta\nu}{2\pi}}{\left(\frac{\Delta\nu}{2}\right)^2 + (\nu - \nu_0')^2} \right] dN}{1 + \frac{\Phi}{\Phi_s} \cdot \frac{\left(\frac{\Delta\nu}{2}\right)^2}{\left(\frac{\Delta\nu}{2}\right)^2 + (\nu - \nu_0')^2}} \quad\Rightarrow\quad d\gamma = \frac{\frac{\lambda^2}{8\pi\tau_{\mathrm{sp}}} \left(\frac{\Delta\nu}{2\pi}\right) dN}{\left(\frac{\Delta\nu}{2}\right)^2 + (\nu - \nu_0')^2 + \frac{\Phi}{\Phi_s}\left(\frac{\Delta\nu}{2}\right)^2}
\end{equation*}
Grouping the terms involving $\left(\frac{\Delta\nu}{2}\right)^2$ in the denominator yields:
\begin{equation*}
    d\gamma = \frac{\frac{\lambda^2}{8\pi\tau_{\mathrm{sp}}} \left(\frac{\Delta\nu}{2\pi}\right) dN}{\left(\frac{\Delta\nu}{2}\right)^2\left(1 + \frac{\Phi}{\Phi_s}\right) + (\nu - \nu_0')^2}
\end{equation*}

The photon flux $\Phi$ expands the unperturbed linewidth term $\left(\frac{\Delta\nu}{2}\right)^2$ into an intensity-dependent effective width. This phenomenon is known as \textbf{power broadening}. To cast the saturated lineshape back into standard Lorentzian form, we define the \textbf{power-broadened linewidth} $\Delta\nu_s$:
\begin{equation*}
    \left(\frac{\Delta\nu_s}{2}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{\Delta\nu}{2}\right)^2\left(1 + \frac{\Phi}{\Phi_s}\right) \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{\Delta\nu_s}{2} = \left(\frac{\Delta\nu}{2}\right)\sqrt{1 + \frac{\Phi}{\Phi_s}}
\end{equation*}
Taking the positive square root yields the final relation:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        \Delta\nu_s = \Delta\nu\sqrt{1 + \frac{\Phi}{\Phi_s}}
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

Substituting $\Delta\nu_s$ into the denominator, we multiply and divide the numerator by $\Delta\nu_s$ to extract a normalised Lorentzian lineshape:
\begin{equation*}
    d\gamma = \frac{\frac{\lambda^2}{8\pi\tau_{\mathrm{sp}}} \left(\frac{\Delta\nu}{2\pi}\right) dN}{\left(\frac{\Delta\nu_s}{2}\right)^2 + (\nu - \nu_0')^2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad d\gamma = \frac{\lambda^2}{8\pi\tau_{\mathrm{sp}}} \left[ \frac{\Delta\nu}{\Delta\nu_s} \cdot \frac{\frac{\Delta\nu_s}{2\pi}}{\left(\frac{\Delta\nu_s}{2}\right)^2 + (\nu - \nu_0')^2} \right] dN
\end{equation*}

Where the scaling factor $\frac{\Delta\nu}{\Delta\nu_s} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + \frac{\Phi}{\Phi_s}}}$ represents the local gain suppression due to saturation.

\subsubsection*{Averaging Over the Inhomogeneous Distribution}

To find the total macroscopic saturated gain $\bar{\gamma}$ of the entire medium, we must integrate the saturated gain contributions $d\gamma$ from all velocity classes. This is accomplished by weighting each homogeneous velocity class by its physical population density. In a gas laser, the velocity $V$ of active atoms along the cavity axis follows a standard Maxwell-Boltzmann Gaussian probability distribution.

The differential population inversion density $dN$ contributed by the specific subset of active atoms moving with a velocity between $V$ and $V+dV$ is simply the total macroscopic inversion $N_0$ weighted by the fraction of atoms in that velocity class, yielding $dN = N_0 f_V(V) \, dV$. Here, $N_0$ is the total, unsaturated population inversion density of the entire gas medium, and $f_V(V) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_V} \exp\!\left(-\frac{V^2}{2\sigma_V^2}\right)$ is the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution governing the thermal speeds of the gas atoms along the cavity axis, with $\sigma_V = \sqrt{k_BT/m}$ representing the standard deviation of the velocity distribution.

Substituting this into the saturated gain expression, the total saturated gain at laser frequency $\nu$ is the integral over all velocity classes:
\begin{equation*}
    \bar{\gamma} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{\lambda^2}{8\pi\tau_{\mathrm{sp}}} N_0 \frac{\Delta\nu}{\Delta\nu_s} \left[ \frac{\frac{\Delta\nu_s}{2\pi}}{\left(\frac{\Delta\nu_s}{2}\right)^2 + \left(\nu - \nu_0\left[1+\frac{V}{c}\right]\right)^2} \right] \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_V} \exp\!\left(-\frac{V^2}{2\sigma_V^2}\right) \, dV \tag{I}
\end{equation*}

To evaluate this integral, we examine the \textbf{extreme inhomogeneous limit}, where the macroscopic Doppler frequency spread $\sigma_D$ is vastly wider than the power-broadened homogeneous linewidth $\Delta\nu_s$ (i.e., $\sigma_D \gg \Delta\nu_s$, where $\sigma_D \equiv \frac{\nu_0}{c}\sigma_V$). In this regime, the normalised Lorentzian lineshape behaves virtually as a Dirac delta function. Using the Cauchy limit identity:
\begin{equation*}
    \lim_{\epsilon \to 0} \frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon^2 + x^2} = \pi\,\delta(x)
\end{equation*}
with $\epsilon = \frac{\Delta\nu_s}{2}$ and $x = \nu - \nu_0 - \nu_0\left(\frac{V}{c}\right)$, the collapsing lineshape yields:
\begin{equation*}
    \lim_{\Delta\nu_s \to 0} \left[ \frac{\frac{\Delta\nu_s}{2\pi}}{\left(\frac{\Delta\nu_s}{2}\right)^2 + \left(\nu - \nu_0 - \nu_0\left(\frac{V}{c}\right)\right)^2} \right] = \delta\!\left(\nu - \nu_0 - \nu_0\left(\frac{V}{c}\right)\right)
\end{equation*}

Substituting this Dirac delta approximation into our macroscopic gain integral \textup{(I)}, and factoring out the velocity-independent terms, we obtain:
\begin{equation*}
    \bar{\gamma} \approx \frac{\lambda^2}{8\pi\tau_{\mathrm{sp}}} N_0 \frac{\Delta\nu}{\Delta\nu_s} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \delta\!\left(\nu - \nu_0 - \nu_0\left(\frac{V}{c}\right)\right) \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_V} \exp\!\left(-\frac{V^2}{2\sigma_V^2}\right) \, dV
\end{equation*}

To perform the integration using the sifting property of the delta function, we change variables to clear the coefficient inside the delta function's argument. Let us define a new dummy integration variable $u$:
\begin{equation*}
    u \equiv \frac{\nu_0}{c}\,V \quad\Rightarrow\quad V = \frac{c}{\nu_0}\,u \quad\text{and}\quad dV = \frac{c}{\nu_0}\,du
\end{equation*}
Substituting these into the integral yields:
\begin{equation*}
    \bar{\gamma} \approx \frac{\lambda^2}{8\pi\tau_{\mathrm{sp}}} N_0 \frac{\Delta\nu}{\Delta\nu_s} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \delta(\nu - \nu_0 - u) \cdot \left[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_V} \exp\!\left(-\frac{c^2 u^2}{2\nu_0^2\sigma_V^2}\right) \right] \frac{c}{\nu_0}\,du
\end{equation*}

Using the symmetry of the delta function ($\delta(\nu - \nu_0 - u) = \delta(u - (\nu - \nu_0))$), the sifting property collapses the integral:
\begin{equation*}
    \bar{\gamma} \approx \frac{\Delta\nu}{\Delta\nu_s} \cdot \left[ \frac{\lambda^2}{8\pi\tau_{\mathrm{sp}}} N_0 \cdot \frac{c}{\nu_0} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_V} \exp\!\left(-\frac{c^2(\nu - \nu_0)^2}{2\nu_0^2\sigma_V^2}\right) \right]
\end{equation*}
Since the linewidth ratio is $\frac{\Delta\nu}{\Delta\nu_s} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + \frac{\Phi}{\Phi_s}}}$, it evaluates to $1$ in the small-signal limit ($\Phi \to 0$). In this unperturbed state, the bracketed expression represents the purely unsaturated inhomogeneous gain profile $\bar{\gamma}_0$. By defining the Doppler broadening parameter $\sigma_D \equiv \frac{\nu_0}{c}\sigma_V$, we obtain the classic Gaussian envelope:
\begin{equation*}
    \bar{\gamma}_0 = \frac{\lambda^2}{8\pi\tau_{\mathrm{sp}}} N_0 \cdot \frac{c}{\nu_0} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_V} \exp\!\left(-\frac{c^2(\nu - \nu_0)^2}{2\nu_0^2\sigma_V^2}\right) \quad\Rightarrow\quad \bar{\gamma}_0 = \frac{\lambda^2}{8\pi\tau_{\mathrm{sp}}} N_0 \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_D} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(\nu - \nu_0)^2}{2\sigma_D^2}\right)
\end{equation*}

Substituting this unperturbed $\bar{\gamma}_0$ profile back into the collapsed gain equation yields the final inhomogeneous gain saturation law:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        \bar{\gamma} = \frac{\Delta\nu}{\Delta\nu_s} \bar{\gamma}_0 = \frac{\bar{\gamma}_0}{\sqrt{1 + \frac{\Phi}{\Phi_s}}} \quad \text{where} \quad \bar{\gamma}_0 = \frac{\lambda^2}{8\pi\tau_{\mathrm{sp}}} N_0 \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_D} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(\nu - \nu_0)^2}{2\sigma_D^2}\right)
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

\begin{keyinsight}[Homogeneous vs.\ Inhomogeneous Saturation]
    This result exposes a fundamental physical contrast: homogeneous media saturate globally as $(1+\Phi/\Phi_s)^{-1}$ because every atom is identical (requiring exactly $\Phi = \Phi_s$ to halve the gain), whereas inhomogeneous media saturate locally as $(1+\Phi/\Phi_s)^{-1/2}$. Because the optical field only depletes the specific velocity class in resonance, leaving the rest of the distribution untouched, the overall macroscopic gain drops much more slowly. Halving the overall gain in an inhomogeneous medium thus requires an intensity of $\Phi = 3\Phi_s$---exactly \textbf{three times} the photon flux required by its homogeneous counterpart.
\end{keyinsight}

\subsection{Spectral Hole Burning}

Because gain saturation in an inhomogeneous medium is strictly \emph{local} in the frequency domain, a lasing mode at frequency $\nu_q$ interacts only with the specific sub-group of atoms it resonates with. It depletes the inversion of these atoms alone, leaving atoms at other frequencies entirely unaffected and at their full small-signal inversion.

As the mode intensifies and saturates this sub-group, it physically carves a narrow depression into the broad inhomogeneous gain envelope. This localized depletion is known as \textbf{spectral hole burning}. The spectral width of the burned hole corresponds precisely to the power-broadened homogeneous linewidth of that specific atomic sub-group: $\Delta\nu_s = \Delta\nu\sqrt{1 + \frac{\Phi}{\Phi_s}}$.

This localized saturation fundamentally alters multi-mode behavior. Because different longitudinal modes interact with different sub-groups of atoms, their saturation dynamics are completely decoupled. Burning a hole at $\nu_q$ does not reduce the gain available for an adjacent mode at $\nu_{q+1}$. Consequently, inhomogeneously broadened lasers natively support \textbf{multi-mode oscillation}---multiple modes can lase simultaneously, each drawing power from its own private pool of resonant atoms. The number of oscillating modes is roughly $B/\nu_F$, in stark contrast to homogeneously broadened lasers where global saturation forces single-mode operation.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{Figures/Chapter 4/ihb_spectral_hole_burning.jpg}
    \caption{Inhomogeneous Broadening --- Spectral Hole Burning: (Left) The broad inhomogeneous gain envelope comprises many independent, narrow homogeneous sub-groups. (Right) Oscillating modes deplete only their resonant sub-groups, burning isolated spectral holes down to the cavity loss line $\alpha_r$. Because the remaining profile is untouched, multiple modes can lase simultaneously without competition.}

When observing the hole-burned profile in the figure above, two features are physically significant. First, the \textbf{red dots} represent the exact steady-state clamping points where the saturated gain equals the cavity loss ($\gamma = \alpha_r$), enabling continuous oscillation. Second, the \textbf{shaded region} is the literal ``spectral hole''---it represents the macroscopic inversion that has been depleted by the intense optical field. The width of this depleted region is the power-broadened linewidth $\Delta\nu_s$, which is noticeably wider than the natural unperturbed sub-groups ($\Delta\nu$) due to the strong intracavity photon flux.

\subsection{Steady-State Intracavity Photon Flux}

Having established the inhomogeneous gain saturation law, we can now determine the steady-state intracavity photon flux. Just as in the homogeneously broadened case, continuous oscillation requires the saturated gain to lock exactly to the cavity loss at steady state. Setting $\bar{\gamma} = \alpha_r$:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\bar{\gamma}_0}{\sqrt{1 + \frac{\Phi}{\Phi_s}}} = \alpha_r
\end{equation*}

Solving for the intracavity photon-flux density $\Phi$:
\begin{equation*}
    \sqrt{1 + \frac{\Phi}{\Phi_s}} = \frac{\bar{\gamma}_0}{\alpha_r}
    \quad\Rightarrow\quad
    1 + \frac{\Phi}{\Phi_s} = \left(\frac{\bar{\gamma}_0}{\alpha_r}\right)^{\!2}
    \quad\Rightarrow\quad
    \frac{\Phi}{\Phi_s} = \left(\frac{\bar{\gamma}_0}{\alpha_r}\right)^{\!2} - 1
\end{equation*}

\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        \Phi = \Phi_s \left[ \left(\frac{\bar{\gamma}_0}{\alpha_r}\right)^{\!2} - 1 \right]
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

\subsection{Steady-State Output Power}

To model the practical output of this inhomogeneously broadened laser, we again assume a single output mirror configuration with a highly reflective output coupler ($T = 1 - R \ll 1$). The physical output power exiting the laser is the energy carried by the forward-travelling half of the flux passing through the beam area $A$ and transmitting through $M_2$:
\begin{equation*}
    P_{\text{out}} = \left(\frac{\Phi}{2}\right) h\nu\cdot A\cdot T
\end{equation*}

Substituting our derived steady-state flux $\Phi$:
\begin{equation*}
    P_{\text{out}} = \frac{1}{2} \left[ \Phi_s \left( \left(\frac{\bar{\gamma}_0}{\alpha_r}\right)^{\!2} - 1 \right) \right] h\nu\cdot A\cdot T \quad \Rightarrow \quad P_{\text{out}} = \frac{\Phi_s\,h\nu\,A}{2}\cdot T\left[ \left(\frac{\bar{\gamma}_0}{\alpha_r}\right)^{\!2} - 1 \right]
\end{equation*}

By defining the macroscopic saturation power $P_s = \Phi_s\,h\nu\,A$, we encapsulate the medium's intrinsic saturation properties and beam geometry into a single power term, simplifying the expression to:
\begin{equation*}
    P_{\text{out}} = \frac{P_s}{2}\cdot T\left[ \left(\frac{\bar{\gamma}_0}{\alpha_r}\right)^{\!2} - 1 \right]
\end{equation*}

The total cavity loss coefficient, $\alpha_r$, is the sum of the internal scattering and absorption loss, $\alpha_s$, and the distributed mirror loss. Just as we did for the homogeneously broadened case, for highly reflective mirrors ($R \approx 1$), the transmissivity $T = 1 - R$ is sufficiently small ($T \ll 1$) to justify the Taylor approximation $\ln(1-T) \approx -T$. By substituting this into the mirror loss term, the algebraic steps gracefully simplify:
\begin{equation*}
    \alpha_r = \alpha_s + \frac{1}{2L}\ln\left(\frac{1}{R}\right) \quad \Rightarrow \quad \alpha_r = \alpha_s - \frac{1}{2L}\ln(1-T) \quad \Rightarrow \quad \alpha_r \approx \alpha_s - \frac{1}{2L}(-T) = \alpha_s + \frac{T}{2L}
\end{equation*}

Substituting this explicit loss approximation directly back into our power equation, and multiplying the gain-to-loss ratio by $(2L)^2 / (2L)^2$ to eliminate the per-length dependence:
\begin{equation*}
    P_{\text{out}} = \frac{P_s}{2}\cdot T\left[ \frac{\bar{\gamma}_0^2}{\left(\alpha_s + \dfrac{T}{2L}\right)^{\!2}} - 1 \right]
    \quad\Rightarrow\quad
    P_{\text{out}} = \frac{P_s}{2}\cdot T\left[ \frac{(2\bar{\gamma}_0 L)^2}{(2\alpha_s L + T)^2} - 1 \right]
\end{equation*}

We now recognize the standard unitless macroscopic round-trip variables: the round-trip small-signal inhomogeneous gain $g = 2\bar{\gamma}_0 L$ and the round-trip internal loss $\ell = 2\alpha_s L$. Substituting these definitions transforms the equation into its purely macroscopic form:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        P_{\text{out}} = \frac{P_s}{2}\cdot T\left[ \frac{g^2}{(\ell + T)^2} - 1 \right]
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

Comparing this to the homogeneously broadened output power $P_{\mathrm{hom}} = \frac{P_s}{2}\cdot T \left[ \frac{g}{\ell + T} - 1 \right]$, we can factor the inhomogeneously broadened expression as:
\begin{equation*}
    P_{\text{out}} = \frac{P_s}{2}\cdot T \left( \frac{g}{\ell + T} + 1 \right)\!\left( \frac{g}{\ell + T} - 1 \right)
\end{equation*}
Because the extra factor $( \frac{g}{\ell + T} + 1 )$ is strictly greater than $1$, an inhomogeneously broadened laser extracts \textbf{more output power} than an identical homogeneously broadened laser. Physically, this stems from spectral hole burning. While a homogeneous medium depletes all atoms simultaneously (crashing the global gain rapidly), an inhomogeneous medium only depletes its specific resonant sub-group. This localized depletion causes the macroscopic gain to saturate much more slowly (the square-root dependence). Consequently, the cavity can accumulate a larger photon flux before the gain clamps to the loss line, directly yielding a higher output power.

\begin{keyinsight}[Where is the Optimal Transmission $T_{\text{opt}}$?]
    Unlike a homogeneously broadened laser, which yields a neat closed-form solution ($T_{\text{opt}} = \sqrt{g\ell} - \ell$), taking the derivative of the inhomogeneously broadened power equation $dP_{\text{out}}/dT = 0$ results in a messy cubic equation: $(\ell + T)^3 = g^2(\ell - T)$. While the fundamental physical trade-off between trapping photons and extracting them still exists, finding the exact optimal mirror coupling usually requires solving this cubic equation numerically.
\end{keyinsight}

\subsubsection{Power Distribution Across Modes}

In a multi-mode inhomogeneously broadened laser, longitudinal modes interact with mutually exclusive sub-groups of atoms. Because their saturation dynamics are decoupled, we must evaluate the steady-state conditions on a strict mode-by-mode basis.

Each mode at frequency $\nu_q$ experiences a distinct round-trip small-signal gain $g_q$. Expanding the macroscopic gain definition ($g_q = 2 \, \bar{\gamma}_0 \big|_{\nu = \nu_q} L$) reveals its exact physical dependencies:
\begin{equation*}
    g_q = 2 \left[ \frac{\lambda^2}{8\pi\tau_{\mathrm{sp}}} N_0 \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_D} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(\nu_q - \nu_0)^2}{2\sigma_D^2}\right) \right] L
\end{equation*}
The gain scales linearly with total inversion $N_0$ and cavity length $L$, inversely with Doppler width $\sigma_D$ and spontaneous lifetime $\tau_{\text{sp}}$, and decays as a Gaussian as the mode detunes from the atomic resonance $\nu_0$.

Because the modes saturate their independent atomic pools without cross-competition, the macroscopic output power equation applies individually to every surviving mode. The useful power extracted by a specific mode $q$ is:
\begin{equation*}
    P_{\text{out},q} = \frac{P_s}{2}\cdot T\left[ \frac{g_q^2}{(\ell + T)^2} - 1 \right]
\end{equation*}

This quadratic dependence on $g_q$ directly dictates the shape of the output spectrum. Modes near the center of the bandwidth ($\nu_q \approx \nu_0$) experience the highest gain and dominate the output power. Modes near the edges of the bandwidth ($g_q \approx \ell + T$) barely exceed threshold and emit negligible power.

Consequently, the multi-mode spectrum forms a structured set of discrete lines whose overall intensity envelope follows the \emph{squared} Gaussian gain profile, peaking sharply at $\nu_0$ and tapering smoothly to zero at the bandwidth limits.

\begin{keyinsight}[Inhomogeneously Broadened Output Is Not a Flat Comb]
    It is a common misconception that because all modes survive simultaneously, an inhomogeneously broadened laser outputs a flat frequency comb. In reality, the output power varies dramatically across the spectrum. Central modes saturate the hardest and dominate the output, while edge modes barely emit. The final output spectrum is bounded by a Gaussian envelope, not a rectangular window.
\end{keyinsight}

\section{Spatial Hole Burning}

While the distinction between homogeneous and inhomogeneous broadening describes saturation in \emph{frequency} space, a Fabry-P\'{e}rot cavity intrinsically creates structure in \emph{real} space. The counter-propagating fields of a resonant mode form a standing wave, creating an intensity pattern that alternates between bright peaks (antinodes) and dark nulls (nodes) with a spatial period of $\lambda/2$ along the longitudinal cavity axis.

Because stimulated emission depends directly on local field intensity, the population inversion is not depleted uniformly across the cavity. Instead, the gain is heavily saturated at the antinodes (where intensity is highest) and remains virtually untouched at the nodes (where intensity is zero). The lasing mode effectively burns a spatially periodic grating of depleted atoms into the active medium. This phenomenon is called \textbf{spatial hole burning}.

This spatial structure provides a critical loophole in homogeneously broadened lasers. In theory, a homogeneously broadened medium should oscillate in exactly one longitudinal mode because the strongest mode globally crashes the gain to the loss line. However, a second longitudinal mode at a different frequency $\nu_2$ has a different standing-wave period ($\lambda_2/2$). Because its antinodes do not perfectly align with the antinodes of the first mode, it can position its intensity peaks over the untouched, unsaturated regions (the nodes) left behind by the first mode.

By tapping into these un-depleted spatial pockets of population inversion, the second mode can experience enough small-signal gain to reach threshold and lase. Consequently, spatial hole burning fundamentally weakens global mode competition, allowing multiple longitudinal modes to coexist even in a purely homogeneously broadened laser.

\section{The Fabry-P\'{e}rot Resonator and Amplifier}

The round-trip phase and amplitude conditions establish the threshold for self-sustained laser oscillation, but they do not describe the cavity's frequency-resolving power or its response to external signals in the sub-threshold regime. A complete description requires analyzing the Fabry-P\'{e}rot cavity through formal electromagnetic wave theory.

\subsection{Operating Regimes of a Fabry-P\'{e}rot Cavity}

It is a profound realization of laser physics that the Fabry-P\'{e}rot resonator, the Fabry-P\'{e}rot amplifier, and the laser oscillator are not three different devices --- they are the exact same physical device. Because the threshold loss $\alpha_r$ is permanently fixed by the mirrors and the cavity geometry, the physical behavior of the device is dictated entirely by a single remaining independent variable: the \textbf{small-signal gain} $\gamma_0$ (the unsaturated gain dictated solely by the pump power). Turning the pump dial up from zero simply marches the identical device through three distinct operational regimes:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Fabry-P\'{e}rot Resonator} ($\gamma_0 = 0$): Without any pump power, the cavity cannot generate optical power. It functions purely as an optical filter. If an external broadband light source is injected, the mirrors trap and selectively transmit only those specific frequencies that constructively interfere, rejecting the rest.

    \item \textbf{Fabry-P\'{e}rot Amplifier} ($0 < \gamma_0 < \alpha_r$): The pump provides some gain, but strictly below the cavity losses. The light cannot self-sustain and will eventually die out if left alone. However, if an external optical signal is injected, the sub-threshold gain multiplies the signal during each round trip, resulting in highly selective amplification of the resonant frequencies.

    \item \textbf{Laser Oscillator} ($\gamma_0 \geq \alpha_r$): The pump provides enough small-signal gain to balance or exceed the cavity losses, making round-trip amplification completely self-sustaining. The device no longer requires an external injected signal; it generates its own coherent light from spontaneous emission. While the pump \emph{attempts} to provide $\gamma_0 \geq \alpha_r$, gain saturation immediately clamps the actual steady-state gain $\gamma$ to exactly $\alpha_r$. (This is the regime whose fundamental threshold conditions were established earlier).
\end{enumerate}

\begin{keyinsight}[Which Regime is the "Laser"?]
    It is crucial to recognize that both the \textbf{Fabry-P\'{e}rot resonator} and the \textbf{Fabry-P\'{e}rot amplifier} fundamentally require an external beam of light injected into them to produce an output. Without an external signal, they produce nothing (or just negligible spontaneous noise).

    The \textbf{laser oscillator}, on the other hand, is the only regime capable of taking its own internal spontaneous emission and amplifying it into a massive, self-sustaining coherent beam without any external optical input. Therefore, when we colloquially refer to a "Laser" (such as a laser pointer or an industrial cutting laser), we are specifically referring to a \textbf{laser oscillator}.
\end{keyinsight}

\subsection{General Theory of the Fabry-P\'{e}rot Cavity}

As established, the Fabry-P\'{e}rot resonator and the Fabry-P\'{e}rot amplifier are identically the same physical device. The only difference is whether the pump is turned off (resulting in zero gain, $\gamma = 0$) or turned on (providing active amplification, $\gamma > 0$). To reflect this, we will first derive the generalized gain and its associated quantities for the cavity using an arbitrary actual gain $\gamma$. Once we have this universal mathematical framework, we can simply plug in $\gamma = 0$ to model the passive filter, or $\gamma > 0$ to model the active amplifier.

\subsubsection{The Generalized Gain}

To find the generalized gain $G = P_{\text{out}}/P_{\text{in}} = |E_{\text{out}}|^2/|E_{\text{in}}|^2$ of the cavity, we track the electric field self-consistently. Let $A(z)$ denote the rightward-travelling field amplitude and $B(z)$ the leftward-travelling one. We inject an external signal field $E_{\text{in}}$ through the front mirror:

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{Figures/Chapter 4/fp_cavity_fields.jpg}
    \caption{Self-consistent fields inside the Fabry-P\'{e}rot cavity. The rightward-travelling field $A(z)$ and leftward-travelling field $B(z)$ are evaluated at the boundaries $z=0$ and $z=L$ to find the overall power transfer function.}
\end{figure}

As either field propagates a single pass across the cavity (from $z=0$ to $z=L$ or vice versa), it acquires a phase delay $e^{-j\beta L}$ and undergoes a net amplitude change $e^{(\gamma-\alpha_s)L/2}$ from simultaneous gain and internal scattering loss. The exponent is halved because $\gamma$ and $\alpha_s$ are \emph{power} coefficients, and the field amplitude is the square root of power:
\begin{equation}
    A(L) = A(0)\,e^{-j\beta L}\,e^{(\gamma-\alpha_s)L/2} \tag{I}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    B(0) = B(L)\,e^{-j\beta L}\,e^{(\gamma-\alpha_s)L/2} \tag{II}
\end{equation}

At the left entrance facet ($z = 0$), the rightward field is the superposition of the transmitted input and the reflected fraction of the leftward field:
\begin{equation}
    A(0) = E_{\text{in}}\,t_1 + B(0)\,r_1 \tag{III}
\end{equation}

At the right exit facet ($z = L$), the new leftward field is the reflected fraction of the rightward field, and the output field is the transmitted fraction:
\begin{equation}
    B(L) = A(L)\,r_2 \tag{IV}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    E_{\text{out}} = A(L)\,t_2 \tag{V}
\end{equation}

We now have five equations and five unknowns ($A(0),\,A(L),\,B(0),\,B(L),\,E_{\text{out}}$). Our goal is to eliminate all internal fields and express $E_{\text{out}}/E_{\text{in}}$ in closed form.

From \textup{(III)}, we first rearrange to isolate the input field:
\begin{equation*}
    E_{\text{in}}\,t_1 = A(0) - B(0)\,r_1 \quad \Rightarrow \quad E_{\text{in}} = \frac{A(0) - B(0)\,r_1}{t_1}
\end{equation*}
Dividing \textup{(V)} by this expression yields:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{E_{\text{out}}}{E_{\text{in}}} = \frac{A(L)\,t_2}{\displaystyle \frac{A(0)-B(0)\,r_1}{t_1}} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \frac{E_{\text{out}}}{E_{\text{in}}} = \frac{A(L)\,t_1\,t_2}{A(0)-B(0)\,r_1} \tag{VI}
\end{equation}

Now, substitute the propagation laws directly into the transfer function. Using \textup{(I)} to replace $A(L)$ in the numerator:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{E_{\text{out}}}{E_{\text{in}}} = \frac{\Bigl[A(0)\,e^{-j\beta L}\,e^{(\gamma-\alpha_s)L/2}\Bigr]\,t_1\,t_2}{A(0)-B(0)\,r_1}
\end{equation*}

For the denominator, we must eliminate $B(0)$. First, we substitute \textup{(IV)} into \textup{(II)} to trace the leftward field backward from the right mirror:
\begin{equation*}
    B(0) = B(L)\,e^{-j\beta L}\,e^{(\gamma-\alpha_s)L/2} \quad \Rightarrow \quad B(0) = \bigl[A(L)\,r_2\bigr]\,e^{-j\beta L}\,e^{(\gamma-\alpha_s)L/2}
\end{equation*}

Next, we substitute \textup{(I)} for $A(L)$ to express this entirely in terms of the initial rightward field $A(0)$:
\begin{equation*}
    B(0) = \Bigl[A(0)\,e^{-j\beta L}\,e^{(\gamma-\alpha_s)L/2}\Bigr]\,r_2\,e^{-j\beta L}\,e^{(\gamma-\alpha_s)L/2} \quad \Rightarrow \quad B(0) = A(0)\,r_2\,e^{-j2\beta L}\,e^{(\gamma-\alpha_s)L}
\end{equation*}

Substituting this explicit form of $B(0)$ back into our transfer function:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{E_{\text{out}}}{E_{\text{in}}} = \frac{A(0)\,t_1\,t_2\,e^{-j\beta L}\,e^{(\gamma-\alpha_s)L/2}}{A(0) - \Bigl[A(0)\,r_2\,e^{-j2\beta L}\,e^{(\gamma-\alpha_s)L}\Bigr]\,r_1}
\end{equation*}

Factoring out $A(0)$ in the denominator and cancelling it with the numerator yields the \textbf{complex field transfer function}:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{E_{\text{out}}}{E_{\text{in}}} = \frac{A(0)\,\Bigl[t_1\,t_2\,e^{-j\beta L}\,e^{(\gamma-\alpha_s)L/2}\Bigr]}{A(0)\,\Bigl[1 - r_1 r_2\,e^{-j2\beta L}\,e^{(\gamma-\alpha_s)L}\Bigr]} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \frac{E_{\text{out}}}{E_{\text{in}}} = \frac{t_1\,t_2\,e^{-j\beta L}\,e^{(\gamma-\alpha_s)L/2}}{1-r_1 r_2\,e^{-j2\beta L}\,e^{(\gamma-\alpha_s)L}}
\end{equation*}

\begin{keyinsight}[The Physics of the Denominator]
    The denominator of the transfer function, $1 - r_1 r_2\,e^{-j2\beta L}\,e^{(\gamma-\alpha_s)L}$, is the mathematical heart of resonant enhancement. The subtracted term represents exactly one round trip inside the cavity: double reflection ($r_1 r_2$), net power change ($e^{(\gamma-\alpha_s)L}$), and accumulated phase ($e^{-j2\beta L}$).

    To maximize the output fraction $E_{\text{out}}/E_{\text{in}}$, this complex denominator must be minimized. This happens when the subtracted term is purely real and positive, subtracting directly from 1. By Euler's formula, the phase term $e^{-j2\beta L} = \cos(2\beta L) - j\sin(2\beta L)$ equals exactly 1 only when the round-trip phase $2\beta L$ is a multiple of $2\pi$.

    Physically, this means the wave has travelled an integer number of wavelengths during its round trip. Upon returning, its peaks perfectly align with incoming light, interfering constructively. This shrinks the denominator to its real minimum, causing transmitted power to sharply peak.
\end{keyinsight}

To simplify this expression, we define the \textbf{single-pass gain} $G_0$ as the net power multiplication that the field experiences during one traversal of the cavity:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        G_0 = e^{(\gamma-\alpha_s)L}
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

To simplify the notation, we convert the field coefficients into their power equivalents. For the numerator, we use the fact that $t_i^2 = 1 - R_i$, meaning the product $t_1 t_2$ becomes $\sqrt{(1-R_1)(1-R_2)}$. For the denominator, we define a composite variable for the geometric mean reflectivity $R \equiv r_1 r_2$, which is equivalent to $R = \sqrt{R_1 R_2}$.

Substituting the single-pass gain $G_0$ (noting that $e^{(\gamma-\alpha_s)L/2} = \sqrt{G_0}$) along with our new power variables, we can rewrite the raw transfer function entirely in terms of power reflectivities:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{E_{\text{out}}}{E_{\text{in}}} = \frac{t_1\,t_2\,e^{-j\beta L}\,e^{(\gamma-\alpha_s)L/2}}{1-r_1 r_2\,e^{-j2\beta L}\,e^{(\gamma-\alpha_s)L}} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \frac{E_{\text{out}}}{E_{\text{in}}} = \frac{\sqrt{(1-R_1)(1-R_2)\,G_0}\;e^{-j\beta L}}{1-R\,G_0\,e^{-j2\beta L}}
\end{equation*}

To find the generalized power gain $G = |E_{\text{out}}/E_{\text{in}}|^2$, we take the squared magnitude of the entire transfer function. The numerator simply loses its phase factor and its square root:
\begin{equation*}
    G = \frac{\left|\sqrt{(1-R_1)(1-R_2)\,G_0}\right|^2}{|1 - R\,G_0\,e^{-j2\beta L}|^2} \quad \Rightarrow \quad G = \frac{(1-R_1)(1-R_2)\,G_0}{|1 - R\,G_0\,e^{-j2\beta L}|^2}
\end{equation*}

To evaluate the squared magnitude in the denominator, we expand the complex exponential using Euler's formula $e^{-j2\beta L} = \cos 2\beta L - j\sin 2\beta L$:
\begin{equation*}
    G = \frac{(1-R_1)(1-R_2)\,G_0}{(1 - R\,G_0 \cos 2\beta L)^2 + (R\,G_0 \sin 2\beta L)^2}
\end{equation*}

Expanding the squared terms in the denominator, and then grouping the common $R^2 G_0^2$ terms yields:
\begin{align*}
    G & = \frac{(1-R_1)(1-R_2)\,G_0}{1 - 2 R\,G_0 \cos 2\beta L + R^2 G_0^2 \cos^2 2\beta L + R^2 G_0^2 \sin^2 2\beta L} \\[1em]
    G & = \frac{(1-R_1)(1-R_2)\,G_0}{1 - 2 R\,G_0 \cos 2\beta L + R^2 G_0^2 (\cos^2 2\beta L + \sin^2 2\beta L)}
\end{align*}

First, applying the trigonometric identity $\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x = 1$ simplifies the grouped term to $R^2 G_0^2$. We then add and subtract $2R\,G_0$ in the denominator to forcefully create a perfect square:
\begin{equation*}
    G = \frac{(1-R_1)(1-R_2)\,G_0}{1 - 2 R\,G_0 \cos 2\beta L + R^2 G_0^2} \quad \Rightarrow \quad G = \frac{(1-R_1)(1-R_2)\,G_0}{1 - 2 R\,G_0 + 2 R\,G_0 - 2 R\,G_0 \cos 2\beta L + R^2 G_0^2}
\end{equation*}

We can now group the terms $(1 - 2R\,G_0 + R^2 G_0^2)$ into the perfect square $(1-R\,G_0)^2$, and factor out $2R\,G_0$ from the remaining terms:
\begin{equation*}
    G = \frac{(1-R_1)(1-R_2)\,G_0}{(1-R\,G_0)^2 + 2 R\,G_0 (1 - \cos 2\beta L)}
\end{equation*}

Finally, applying the half-angle identity $1 - \cos 2\beta L = 2 \sin^2 \beta L$ yields the final \textbf{generalized gain equation} (also known as the generalized Airy function):
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        G = \frac{(1-R_1)(1-R_2)\,G_0}{\left(1 - R\,G_0\right)^2 + 4R\,G_0\,\sin^2(\beta L)}
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}
This equation describes the exact power gain lineshape of the cavity. It is mathematically universal for any sub-threshold operation ($0 \leq \gamma < \alpha_r$), seamlessly capturing both operating regimes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Passive Resonator ($\gamma = 0$):} The single-pass gain is $G_0 = e^{-\alpha_s L} \leq 1$. The overall gain $G$ remains below unity, meaning the cavity acts purely as a frequency-selective filter.
    \item \textbf{Active Amplifier ($\gamma > 0$):} The single-pass gain is $G_0 > 1$. The overall gain $G$ can vastly exceed unity, meaning the cavity provides true optical amplification.
\end{itemize}

Furthermore, because the $\sin^2(\beta L)$ term in the denominator oscillates between $0$ and $1$, the generalized gain is highly frequency-dependent. It swings periodically between resonance peaks and anti-resonance troughs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Resonance Maximum:} The gain peaks when the denominator is minimized, which occurs when $\sin^2(\beta L) = 0$. Substituting this into the generalized gain equation yields:
          \begin{equation*}
              G_{\max} = \frac{(1-R_1)(1-R_2)\,G_0}{(1 - R\,G_0)^2 + 4R\,G_0(0)} \quad \Rightarrow \quad G_{\max} = \frac{(1-R_1)(1-R_2)\,G_0}{(1 - R\,G_0)^2}
          \end{equation*}

    \item \textbf{Anti-Resonance Minimum:} The gain drops to a trough when the denominator is maximized, which occurs when $\sin^2(\beta L) = 1$. Substituting this into the generalized gain equation and expanding the perfect square gives:
          \begin{equation*}
              G_{\min} = \frac{(1-R_1)(1-R_2)\,G_0}{(1 - R\,G_0)^2 + 4R\,G_0(1)} \quad \Rightarrow \quad G_{\min} = \frac{(1-R_1)(1-R_2)\,G_0}{1 - 2R\,G_0 + R^2 G_0^2 + 4R\,G_0}
          \end{equation*}
          Combining the $R\,G_0$ terms allows the denominator to explicitly re-factor into the perfect square $(1+R\,G_0)^2$, yielding the final trough value:
          \begin{equation*}
              G_{\min} = \frac{(1-R_1)(1-R_2)\,G_0}{1 + 2R\,G_0 + R^2 G_0^2} \quad \Rightarrow \quad G_{\min} = \frac{(1-R_1)(1-R_2)\,G_0}{(1 + R\,G_0)^2}
          \end{equation*}
\end{itemize}

The severity of this frequency swing is quantified by the \textbf{peak-to-trough ratio} $\rho$:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        \rho = \frac{G_{\max}}{G_{\min}}
        = \left(\frac{1 + R\,G_0}{1 - R\,G_0}\right)^{\!2}
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}
Depending on the operating regime, this universal ratio takes on different physical meanings. In a passive filter ($\gamma = 0$), it is called the \textbf{contrast factor} (or fringe visibility), describing how well the filter rejects off-resonance light. In an active amplifier ($\gamma > 0$), it is called the \textbf{gain ripple}.

\subsubsection{Universal linewidth}

While the peak-to-trough ratio $\rho$ describes the vertical contrast of the resonances, we must also quantify their horizontal sharpness. In a passive cavity, this dictates the frequency selectivity of the filter; in an active cavity, it defines the amplification bandwidth of a single longitudinal mode. Both are measured by the Full Width at Half Maximum (FWHM) linewidth $\delta\nu$.

Because the Fabry-Pérot resonance spikes are much narrower than the active medium's broad gain spectrum, we treat $G_0$ as locally constant across any individual peak. This defines a local $\delta\nu$ for each interference spike, which narrows near the gain center and broadens on the wings. To find $\delta\nu$, we set the generalized gain equal to half its local peak value, $G = \tfrac{1}{2}G_{\max}$:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{(1-R_1)(1-R_2)\,G_0}{\left(1 - R\,G_0\right)^2 + 4R\,G_0\,\sin^2(\beta L)} = \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{(1-R_1)(1-R_2)\,G_0}{\left(1 - R\,G_0\right)^2}
\end{equation*}

Canceling the numerators and inverting both sides, then subtracting $(1 - R\,G_0)^2$ yields:
\begin{equation*}
    \left(1 - R\,G_0\right)^2 + 4R\,G_0\,\sin^2(\beta L) = 2\left(1 - R\,G_0\right)^2 \quad \Rightarrow \quad 4R\,G_0\,\sin^2(\beta L) = \left(1 - R\,G_0\right)^2
\end{equation*}

Dividing by $4R\,G_0$ isolates the sine-squared term. Taking the square root then gives the phase detuning $\beta L$ from resonance at the half-maximum points:
\begin{equation*}
    \sin^2(\beta L) = \frac{(1 - R\,G_0)^2}{4R\,G_0} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \sin(\beta L) = \frac{1 - R\,G_0}{2\sqrt{R\,G_0}} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \beta L = \sin^{-1}\!\left[\frac{1 - R\,G_0}{2\sqrt{R\,G_0}}\right]
\end{equation*}

Using the propagation constant $\beta = 2\pi\nu / c$ (where $c$ is the speed of light in the medium) and the FSR $\nu_F = c / 2L$, the absolute phase relates to absolute frequency via $\beta L = \pi\nu/\nu_F$. Because the inverse sine function mathematically returns a principal value (a small angle), its result inherently isolates the \textit{relative} phase distance from the nearest resonance peak, ignoring the absolute center ($q\pi$). Evaluating this locally relative to a peak center $\nu_q$, the phase detuning relates directly to the half-maximum frequency distance $\nu_{\text{HM}}$:
\begin{equation*}
    \beta L = \pi \frac{\nu_{\text{HM}}}{\nu_F} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \nu_{\text{HM}} = \frac{\nu_F}{\pi}(\beta L) \quad \Rightarrow \quad \nu_{\text{HM}} = \frac{\nu_F}{\pi} \sin^{-1}\!\left[\frac{1 - R\,G_0}{2\sqrt{R\,G_0}}\right]
\end{equation*}

Because the $\sin^2$ function is symmetric around its resonance zeros, the gain peak is perfectly symmetric. Doubling the half-maximum detuning ($\delta\nu = 2\nu_{\text{HM}}$) gives the exact full-width universal linewidth:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        \delta\nu = \frac{2\,\nu_F}{\pi}\,
        \sin^{-1}\!\left[\frac{1 - R\,G_0}{2\sqrt{R\,G_0}}\right]
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

Because $G_0$ is embedded in this equation, the linewidth is inherently frequency-dependent. This dependency manifests differently depending on whether the device is a passive filter (where $G_0$ is a constant loss) or an active amplifier (where $G_0$ follows the medium's gain spectrum). Crucially, this macroscopic derivation assumes sub-threshold operation ($R\,G_0 < 1$). As $R\,G_0 \to 1$, the predicted linewidth collapses toward zero, marking the physical transition from an amplifier into a coherent laser oscillator.

\subsubsection{General finesse}

While the linewidth $\delta\nu$ dictates the absolute frequency width of a longitudinal mode, it is heavily dependent on the physical length of the cavity (since it is proportional to $\nu_F = c/2L$). To obtain a pure, length-independent metric of the cavity's trapping quality---one dictated solely by the mirror reflectivity and internal gain---we define the \textbf{finesse} $\mathcal{F}$ as the ratio of the peak spacing (FSR) to the peak width:
\begin{equation*}
    \mathcal{F} = \frac{\nu_F}{\delta\nu}
\end{equation*}

To find the general expression for finesse, we substitute the exact universal linewidth formula we just derived into this definition. The $\nu_F$ terms immediately cancel, removing the length dependency:
\begin{equation*}
    \mathcal{F} = \frac{\nu_F}{\frac{2\,\nu_F}{\pi} \sin^{-1}\!\left[\frac{1 - R\,G_0}{2\sqrt{R\,G_0}}\right]} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathcal{F} = \frac{\pi}{2\,\sin^{-1}\!\left[\frac{1 - R\,G_0}{2\sqrt{R\,G_0}}\right]}
\end{equation*}

In practice, useful optical cavities have sharp peaks where the linewidth is much smaller than the FSR ($\delta\nu \ll \nu_F$). In this high-resolution limit, the argument of the inverse sine is small, allowing us to use the small-angle approximation $\sin^{-1}(x) \approx x$. Applying this to the exact finesse yields the standard approximation:
\begin{equation*}
    \mathcal{F} \approx \frac{\pi}{2 \left( \frac{1 - R\,G_0}{2\sqrt{R\,G_0}} \right)} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathcal{F}_{\text{general}} \approx \frac{\pi\sqrt{R\,G_0}}{1 - R\,G_0}
\end{equation*}

\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{F}_{\text{general}} \approx \frac{\pi\sqrt{R\,G_0}}{1 - R\,G_0}
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

Notice the $(1 - R\,G_0)$ term in the denominator. Physically, $R\,G_0$ is the fraction of energy that survives a single round trip, making $(1 - R\,G_0)$ the net fractional loss. Because finesse scales inversely with this net loss, it physically represents the effective number of round trips a photon completes before leaving the cavity. It plays the exact same conceptual role as the quality factor $Q$ in an electronic circuit: as the system approaches the lasing threshold ($R\,G_0 \to 1$), the net loss vanishes, the trapped photons survive indefinitely, and the finesse diverges to infinity.

\subsection{Regime 1: The Passive Fabry-P\'{e}rot Resonator (\texorpdfstring{$\gamma = 0$}{gamma = 0})}

If we turn off the pump ($\gamma = 0$), the device operates purely as a passive optical filter governed by multi-beam interference. When illuminated by a broad spectrum, the cavity only transmits wavelengths that satisfy the resonance condition: a round-trip phase shift equal to a multiple of $2\pi$. Physically, this means the cavity length $L$ must fit an exact integer number $q$ of half-wavelengths ($L = q\lambda/2$). Wavelengths meeting this condition build up via constructive interference and emerge as discrete longitudinal modes. Off-resonance wavelengths suffer destructive interference and are reflected. Because the passive internal loss is uniform across all frequencies, every transmitted mode $q$ reaches the exact same peak amplitude---a defining characteristic that sharply contrasts with the frequency-dependent peaks of an active amplifier.

Mathematically, setting $\gamma = 0$ removes all active amplification, collapsing the single-pass gain factor strictly to the medium's passive exponential attenuation: $G_0 = e^{-\alpha_s L}$. Because this internal loss ($\alpha_s$) is frequency-independent, substituting this constant factor into our generalized master equation immediately reduces the active power gain $G$ into the passive fractional transmission spectrum $T$, known as the Airy function:
\begin{equation*}
    G = \frac{(1-R_1)(1-R_2)\,G_0}{\left(1 - R\,G_0\right)^2 + 4R\,G_0\,\sin^2(\beta L)} \quad \Rightarrow \quad T = \frac{(1-R_1)(1-R_2)\,e^{-\alpha_s L}}{\left(1 - R\,e^{-\alpha_s L}\right)^2 + 4R\,e^{-\alpha_s L}\,\sin^2(\beta L)}
\end{equation*}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{Figures/Chapter 4/airy_function_spectrum.jpg}
    \caption{The Airy transmission function of a passive Fabry-P\'{e}rot resonator. Because the internal loss $G_0$ is constant, every resonance peak experiences the exact same attenuation, forming an infinite, perfectly periodic comb of transmitted frequencies.}
\end{figure}

This transmission spectrum forms an infinite periodic comb of identical resonance spikes, allowing only discrete, equally spaced frequency channels to pass through. To evaluate the resolving sharpness of these passive transmission peaks, we specialize the general finesse expression $\mathcal{F}_{\text{general}} \approx \pi\sqrt{R\,G_0}/(1 - R\,G_0)$ by setting $G_0 = e^{-\alpha_s L}$. Accounting for unequal mirrors ($R_1 \neq R_2$) and non-zero internal scattering or absorption loss ($\alpha_s > 0$), the exact passive finesse becomes:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{F}_{\text{passive}} \approx \frac{\pi\sqrt{R\,e^{-\alpha_s L}}}{1 - R\,e^{-\alpha_s L}}
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}
In the idealized limit of a lossless internal medium ($\alpha_s = 0$), this expression simplifies to the mirror-limited finesse:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{F}_{\text{mirror}} \approx \frac{\pi\sqrt{R}}{1 - R}
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}
This represents the absolute upper bound of the cavity's resolving power, bounded purely by the mirror reflectivities.

More practically, we can relate the finesse directly to the cavity's total distributed loss. Starting with the exact definition featuring both mirrors, we absorb the $1/2$ factor into the logarithm to recover the geometric mean $R = \sqrt{R_1 R_2}$:
\begin{equation*}
    \alpha_r = \alpha_s + \frac{1}{2L}\ln\left(\frac{1}{R_1 R_2}\right) \quad \Rightarrow \quad \alpha_r = \alpha_s + \frac{1}{L}\ln\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{R_1 R_2}}\right) \quad \Rightarrow \quad \alpha_r = \alpha_s - \frac{1}{L}\ln(R)
\end{equation*}
To smoothly inject this into the finesse formula, we multiply by $L$ and negate both sides to prepare for exponentiation:
\begin{equation*}
    \alpha_r L = \alpha_s L - \ln(R) \quad \Rightarrow \quad -\alpha_r L = \ln(R) - \alpha_s L
\end{equation*}
By exponentiating both sides, we uncover a direct physical identity linking the total exponential distributed loss ($e^{-\alpha_r L}$) to the explicit single-pass cavity attenuation ($R\,e^{-\alpha_s L}$):
\begin{equation*}
    e^{-\alpha_r L} = e^{\ln(R)}\,e^{-\alpha_s L} \quad \Rightarrow \quad e^{-\alpha_r L} = R\,e^{-\alpha_s L}
\end{equation*}
Substituting this identity into our exact passive finesse equation elegantly absorbs the mirror reflectivities into a single exponential term:
\begin{equation*}
    \mathcal{F} \approx \frac{\pi\sqrt{R\,e^{-\alpha_s L}}}{1 - R\,e^{-\alpha_s L}} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathcal{F} \approx \frac{\pi\sqrt{e^{-\alpha_r L}}}{1 - e^{-\alpha_r L}}
\end{equation*}
For low-loss cavities ($\alpha_r L \ll 1$), we can linearize the exponential terms using a first-order Taylor expansion ($e^{-x} \approx 1 - x$). This approximation effortlessly reduces the denominator to a linear loss term, $1 - e^{-\alpha_r L} \approx 1 - (1 - \alpha_r L) = \alpha_r L$. Simultaneously, applying the expansion to the numerator yields $\sqrt{e^{-\alpha_r L}} = e^{-\alpha_r L / 2} \approx 1 - \alpha_r L / 2$. Because the fractional loss $\alpha_r L$ is exceedingly small, this slight reduction is completely negligible compared to $1$, leaving the numerator as simply the constant $\pi$. Combining these two limits, the exact finesse elegantly simplifies to:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{F} \approx \frac{\pi}{\alpha_r L} \qquad (\alpha_r L \ll 1)
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}
This approximation reveals the physical essence of the passive cavity: the finesse is simply $\pi$ divided by the total fractional round-trip loss. High finesse demands an extremely low round-trip loss, forcing trapped photons to bounce back and forth many times before leaking out.

\subsubsection{Photon Lifetime and Temporal Dynamics}

While finesse and linewidth describe the cavity's resolving power in the frequency domain, understanding the transient behavior of the cavity requires us to shift into the time domain. It is crucial to define a temporal metric to quantify how long the cavity can trap light, as this ultimately dictates the maximum modulation speeds and switching dynamics of optical systems built upon it.

This trapping capacity is naturally quantified by the \textbf{photon lifetime} $\tau_{\text{ph}}$---the average time a photon survives inside the cavity before escaping through the mirrors or being absorbed. Inside a lossy passive cavity, the optical intensity decays spatially under the total distributed loss as $I(z) = I(0)\,e^{-\alpha_r z}$. By differentiating this expression with respect to position $z$, we obtain the spatial rate of decay:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{dI}{dz} = -\alpha_r\,I(0)\,e^{-\alpha_r z} = -\alpha_r I
\end{equation*}
To project this spatial decay into a temporal decay, we apply the chain rule using the group velocity of the photons, $v_g = dz/dt = c$ (where $c$ is the speed of light in the medium):
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{dI}{dt} = \frac{dI}{dz}\cdot\frac{dz}{dt} = (-\alpha_r I)\cdot c = -\alpha_r c\,I
\end{equation*}
This forms a first-order separable ordinary differential equation. We can solve it by separating the variables $I$ and $t$, and integrating from an initial state at $t=0$:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{dI}{I} = -\alpha_r c\, dt \quad \Rightarrow \quad \int_{I(0)}^{I(t)} \frac{dI'}{I'} = -\alpha_r c \int_0^t dt' \quad \Rightarrow \quad \ln\left(\frac{I(t)}{I(0)}\right) = -\alpha_r c\,t
\end{equation*}
Exponentiating both sides completely unpacks the temporal intensity profile:
\begin{equation*}
    e^{\ln\left(\frac{I(t)}{I(0)}\right)} = e^{-\alpha_r c\,t} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \frac{I(t)}{I(0)} = e^{-\alpha_r c\,t} \quad \Rightarrow \quad I(t) = I(0)\,e^{-\alpha_r c\,t}
\end{equation*}
Comparing this derived intensity function with the standard exponential decay model $I(t) = I(0)\,e^{-t/\tau_{\text{ph}}}$ allows us to directly extract the characteristic photon lifetime:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        \tau_{\text{ph}} = \frac{1}{\alpha_r\,c}
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

Through the Fourier time-frequency uncertainty relation of a decaying exponential, this finite photon lifetime represents the fundamental physical mechanism behind the spectral broadening of the longitudinal modes, directly dictating their FWHM linewidth:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        \delta\nu = \frac{1}{2\pi\,\tau_{\text{ph}}} = \frac{\alpha_r\,c}{2\pi}
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

It is crucial to explicitly recognize that this $\delta\nu$ represents the \textit{passive} (or ``cold'') cavity linewidth. Establishing this fundamental baseline is vital because, as we will soon see, introducing active optical gain into the resonator will dramatically compress this spectral width far below this passive limit.

Ultimately, the physical significance of the photon lifetime extends far beyond a passive decay constant; it dictates the fundamental ON/OFF switching dynamics and maximum modulation bandwidth of any optical system built on this resonator. Depending on the operational state, the photon lifetime imposes strict temporal bottlenecks:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Turn-Off Decay:} When the pump is abruptly turned off, the trapped photons do not vanish instantly. They continue to leak through the mirrors, decaying exponentially as $I(t) = I(0)\,e^{-t/\tau_{\text{ph}}}$. A long photon lifetime severely slows down the cavity's turn-off speed.
    \item \textbf{Turn-On Build-Up Delay:} When the pump is turned on, the system experiences a finite delay. Spontaneous emission photons must travel back and forth, accumulating enough round-trip passes to surpass losses and eventually establish the steady-state macroscopic field.
    \item \textbf{High-Speed Modulation Trade-Off:} For high-speed digital communications, designers must intentionally decrease $\tau_{\text{ph}}$ to facilitate rapid switching. This is practically achieved by lowering the mirror reflectivities $R_i$. Physically, lower reflectivities allow trapped photons to leak out of the cavity faster per round trip. Mathematically, this increases the total distributed loss $\alpha_r$, which inversely shrinks the photon lifetime ($\tau_{\text{ph}} = 1/\alpha_r c$). However, this speed-enhancing compromise inevitably increases the required threshold pump rate $R_{\text{th}}$.
\end{enumerate}
These three cases highlight the fundamental, unavoidable design trade-off between spectral resolution (high finesse, narrow linewidth) and temporal speed in optoelectronic systems.

\subsection{Regime 2: The Fabry-P\'{e}rot Amplifier (\texorpdfstring{$0 < \gamma < \alpha_r$}{0 < gamma < alpha\_r})}

Now we switch on the pump, providing active amplification ($\gamma > 0$). When the single-pass gain surpasses the internal scattering loss ($G_0 = e^{(\gamma - \alpha_s)L} > 1$), the cavity transitions into a power amplifier governed by the generalized active gain:
\begin{equation*}
    G = \frac{(1-R)^2\,G_0}{(1-R\,G_0)^2 + 4R\,G_0\sin^2(\beta L)} \quad \Rightarrow \quad G = \frac{(1-R)^2\,e^{(\gamma - \alpha_s)L}}{\left(1-R\,e^{(\gamma - \alpha_s)L}\right)^2 + 4R\,e^{(\gamma - \alpha_s)L}\sin^2(\beta L)}
\end{equation*}
Unlike the passive filter, this gain $G$ can greatly exceed unity. Because the single-pass gain is inherently frequency-dependent, the theoretical envelopes $G_{\max}$ and $G_{\min}$ are not flat lines; they form broad spectral curves that track the atomic lineshape of the gain medium, bounding the rapid Fabry-P\'{e}rot resonances.

The severity of this oscillation is quantified by the peak-to-trough ratio $\rho$. While this ratio describes fringe contrast in a passive cavity, in an active amplifier it is known as the \textbf{gain ripple}:
\begin{equation*}
    \rho = \frac{G_{\max}}{G_{\min}} = \left(\frac{1 + R\,G_0}{1 - R\,G_0}\right)^2
\end{equation*}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{Figures/Chapter 4/fp_gain_ripple_spectrum.jpg}
    \caption{Gain spectrum of an active Fabry-P\'{e}rot amplifier. Because the single-pass gain $G_0$ is frequency-dependent, the resonance envelope $G_{\max}$ and anti-resonance envelope $G_{\min}$ track the atomic profile. The power gain $G$ oscillates between these bounds, defining the gain ripple $\rho$. At the spectrum tails, as active amplification weakens, the gain envelopes shrink and converge toward the unity gain baseline ($G=1$).}
\end{figure}

In engineering practice, particularly in dense wavelength-division multiplexed (DWDM) optical communication links, this gain ripple must be kept below a strict system-specified bound (often $\rho < 1\,\mathrm{dB}$) to prevent selective, highly unequal amplification of individual wavelength channels.

\begin{keyinsight}[The Travelling-Wave Amplifier (TWA) Limit]
    If we completely remove the cavity mirrors by applying perfect anti-reflective coatings ($R=0$), the ripple factor collapses to unity ($\rho = 1$), representing perfectly flat amplification across all frequencies. In this limit, the generalized gain simplifies to $G = G_0 = e^{(\gamma-\alpha_s)L}$, which is exactly the single-pass gain of a Travelling-Wave Amplifier.

    Furthermore, setting $R=0$ pushes the mirror-loss component to infinity ($\alpha_r \to \infty$). Consequently, the condition $\gamma < \alpha_r$ becomes permanently satisfied for any finite gain. This proves mathematically why a perfectly anti-reflected TWA is locked in the amplifier regime and can never cross into self-sustained oscillation.
\end{keyinsight}

Because active gain physically replenishes the energy lost by a photon during a round trip, it effectively decreases the net cavity loss. This inherently increases the resolving power of the cavity. To evaluate the resolving sharpness of these active resonance peaks, we specialize the general finesse expression $\mathcal{F}_{\text{general}} \approx \pi\sqrt{R\,G_0}/(1 - R\,G_0)$ by setting $G_0 = e^{(\gamma - \alpha_s)L}$. This yields the \textbf{active finesse}:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{F}_{\text{active}} \approx \frac{\pi\sqrt{R\,e^{(\gamma - \alpha_s)L}}}{1 - R\,e^{(\gamma - \alpha_s)L}}
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}
Because $G_0 > 1$, the active finesse is strictly greater than the passive finesse. As the pump is turned up and $G_0$ increases, the transmission peaks become significantly sharper.

\subsubsection{The Oscillation Threshold}

As pump power increases and the round-trip gain perfectly balances the cavity losses ($R\,G_0 \to 1$), the fundamental $(1 - R\,G_0)$ denominator approaches zero. This common term governs three critical amplifier metrics:
\begin{equation*}
    \rho = \left(\frac{1 + R\,G_0}{1 - R\,G_0}\right)^2 \qquad \mathcal{F}_{\text{active}} \approx \frac{\pi\sqrt{R\,G_0}}{1 - R\,G_0} \qquad G_{\max} = \frac{(1-R)^2\,G_0}{(1-R\,G_0)^2}
\end{equation*}
Because this shared denominator vanishes, it forces two simultaneous physical limits:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The gain ripple diverges ($\rho \to \infty$).
    \item The active finesse diverges ($\mathcal{F}_{\text{active}} \to \infty$), ultimately collapsing the resonance linewidth to zero ($\delta\nu \to 0$).
\end{enumerate}

These limits mathematically define the \textbf{oscillation threshold}. At exactly $R\,G_0 = 1$, the peak transmission gain $G_{\max}$ diverges to infinity. Physically, this means that even an infinitesimally small input---such as a single spontaneously emitted photon---will be amplified boundlessly until gain saturation sets in. The device therefore no longer requires a macroscopic external input $P_{\text{in}}$ to produce a coherent output field; it fundamentally transforms from a driven optical amplifier into an active, self-sustaining \textbf{laser oscillator}.

\section{The Hot Resonator: Frequency Pulling and Chirping}

Until now, we have treated the gain medium as a pure amplitude element, assuming the lasing frequencies remain perfectly locked to their cold-cavity geometrical values $\nu_q = qc/(2L)$. However, the gain medium also introduces a frequency-dependent \emph{phase shift}. This is a direct consequence of the Kramers-Kronig relations, which link the real and imaginary parts of the complex susceptibility. We cannot have gain (imaginary part) without an accompanying change in the refractive index (real part). This index change modifies the round-trip phase, pulling the actual oscillation frequencies away from the ideal geometric comb.

\subsection{Kramers-Kronig and Phase Shift from the Gain Medium}

The complex susceptibility of the gain medium has a real part $\chi'$ (dispersion, causes a phase shift) and an imaginary part $\chi''$ (gain or absorption). Close to resonance, these satisfy:
\begin{equation*}
    \chi'' \propto \gamma \qquad\text{(related to gain)}
\end{equation*}
\begin{equation*}
    \chi' = \frac{2(\nu - \nu_0)}{\Delta\nu}\,\chi'' \qquad\text{(Kramers-Kronig near resonance)}
\end{equation*}

The susceptibility $\chi'$ modifies the effective propagation constant. The gain medium adds an extra phase increment $\Delta\beta$ per unit length to the cold-cavity phase constant:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        \Delta\beta = \left(\frac{\nu - \nu_0}{\Delta\nu}\right)\gamma
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

$\Delta\beta = 0$ at line centre $\nu = \nu_0$ --- the gain introduces no extra phase at the atomic resonance itself. Off-resonance, $\Delta\beta$ is proportional to both the detuning from line centre and the gain coefficient.

\subsection{Modified Phase Condition --- The Hot Resonator}

The total propagation constant inside the cavity is now $\beta + \Delta\beta$. The modified round-trip phase condition becomes:
\begin{equation*}
    2(\beta + \Delta\beta)L = 2q\pi
\end{equation*}

Substituting $\beta = 2\pi\nu/c$ and $\Delta\beta$:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{2\nu L}{c} + \frac{(\nu - \nu_0)}{\pi\Delta\nu}\,\gamma L = q
\end{equation*}

The oscillation frequency $\nu'_q$ (the ``hot-resonator'' mode frequency) is the solution of this equation, and in general it differs from the cold-resonator mode $\nu_q = qc/(2L)$.

\subsection{Frequency Pulling}

To understand how $\nu'_q$ differs from $\nu_q$, consider the dispersive profile of $\Delta\beta$: it is negative for $\nu < \nu_0$ (below resonance) and positive for $\nu > \nu_0$ (above resonance). For modes below $\nu_0$, the extra phase \emph{decreases} the effective propagation constant, which makes the cavity look optically shorter --- the mode frequency shifts \emph{up} toward $\nu_0$. For modes above $\nu_0$, the reverse occurs and the mode shifts \emph{down} toward $\nu_0$.

The net effect is that all hot-resonator modes $\nu'_q$ are \textbf{pulled toward the atomic resonance $\nu_0$} relative to the cold-resonator modes $\nu_q$. This phenomenon is called \textbf{frequency pulling}. We can derive the exact pulled frequency explicitly. Recall from the modified phase condition:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{2\nu'_q L}{c} + \frac{(\nu'_q - \nu_0)}{\pi\Delta\nu}\,\gamma L = q
\end{equation*}
The cold-resonator mode satisfies $2\nu_q L/c = q$ exactly, so we substitute $q = 2\nu_q L/c$ and collect terms in $\nu'_q$:
\begin{equation*}
    \nu'_q\!\left[\frac{2L}{c} + \frac{\gamma L}{\pi\Delta\nu}\right] = \frac{2\nu_q L}{c} + \frac{\nu_0\,\gamma L}{\pi\Delta\nu}
\end{equation*}
Dividing through by $L$ and defining the dimensionless pulling parameter $P = c\gamma/(2\pi\Delta\nu)$:
\begin{equation*}
    \nu'_q\!\left[\frac{2}{c} + \frac{\gamma}{\pi\Delta\nu}\right] = \frac{2\nu_q}{c} + \frac{\nu_0\,\gamma}{\pi\Delta\nu}
    \quad\implies\quad
    \nu'_q\,(1 + P) = \nu_q + P\,\nu_0
\end{equation*}
Solving for the hot-resonator mode frequency:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        \nu'_q = \frac{\nu_q + P\,\nu_0}{1 + P}, \qquad P = \frac{c\gamma}{2\pi\,\Delta\nu}
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\nu'_q$: the actual lasing frequency of the $q$-th mode (hot-resonator value).
    \item $\nu_q = qc/(2L)$: the geometric cold-resonator mode frequency.
    \item $P = c\gamma/(2\pi\Delta\nu)$: a dimensionless ratio of gain to atomic linewidth. It quantifies how strongly the gain medium perturbs the cavity modes.
\end{itemize}

The pulling amount --- how far $\nu'_q$ shifts from $\nu_q$ --- is:
\begin{equation*}
    \nu'_q - \nu_q = \frac{P\,(\nu_0 - \nu_q)}{1 + P}
    \xrightarrow{\;P\ll 1\;}
    P\,(\nu_0 - \nu_q) = \frac{c\gamma}{2\pi\Delta\nu}\,(\nu_0 - \nu_q)
\end{equation*}

The pulling is proportional to both the gain coefficient $\gamma$ and the cold-cavity detuning $(\nu_0 - \nu_q)$. Modes already close to line centre ($\nu_q \approx \nu_0$) are barely pulled; modes at the edges of the gain profile feel the strongest pull. In the limit $P \to \infty$ (gain much larger than the linewidth), the formula gives $\nu'_q \to \nu_0$ for every mode --- the entire mode comb would collapse to line centre, a physically unreachable limit that nevertheless confirms the direction of the effect.

    \caption{Frequency Pulling --- Cold vs.~Hot Resonator: The cold-resonator modes (dashed lines) are evenly spaced at the geometric frequencies $\nu_q = qc/(2L)$. \begin{keyinsight}[Cold vs Hot Resonator Frequencies]
    The cold-resonator modes at $\nu_q = qc/(2L)$ are determined purely by cavity geometry. The hot-resonator modes $\nu'_q$ --- the actual oscillation frequencies of the running laser --- are pulled slightly toward the atomic resonance $\nu_0$. The amount of pulling depends on how far $\nu_q$ is from $\nu_0$ and on the magnitude of the gain. This is why a real laser oscillates at frequencies that are close to, but measurably offset from, the purely geometric cold-cavity frequencies. The dispersive Kramers-Kronig phase shift from the gain medium inevitably acts as a force dragging the oscillation toward line centre.
\end{keyinsight}

\subsection{Direct Laser Modulation and Frequency Chirping}

The most practically impactful consequence of frequency pulling arises when the laser is used as a directly modulated transmitter. In direct laser modulation, digital data is encoded by varying the injection current (or pump rate) to swing the output power between a high level (logical~1) and a low level (logical~0). This approach is operationally elegant: no external optical component is required beyond the laser itself.

Frequency pulling, however, immediately identifies a fundamental side-effect. The injection current directly controls the population inversion $N_0$, which sets the gain coefficient $\gamma$. As we have just derived, any change in $\gamma$ simultaneously forces a change in the real part of the susceptibility through the Kramers-Kronig relation, and therefore a change in the intracavity refractive index $n$. A change in $n$ modifies the optical path length inside the cavity, which in turn shifts the resonant mode frequencies. The operating frequency of the laser therefore does not stay fixed during modulation --- it shifts in synchrony with the amplitude.

This unwanted coupling between amplitude modulation and frequency modulation is called \textbf{frequency chirping}. During the rising edge of a transmitted pulse --- when the pump current increases, the gain rises, and the refractive index shifts --- the lasing frequency swings in one direction. During the falling edge, it swings back. The instantaneous optical frequency therefore oscillates up and down in synchrony with the amplitude waveform, broadening the emitted spectrum far beyond what the pure modulation bandwidth alone would predict.

\begin{keyinsight}[Why Chirping Limits Long-Haul Communication]
    A chirped optical pulse carries a range of frequencies that travel at slightly different speeds through a dispersive fibre. This differential propagation speed causes the pulse to spread in time as it travels, eventually causing adjacent pulses to overlap and become indistinguishable at the receiver. The faster the modulation and the more dispersive the fibre, the shorter the usable transmission distance. For this reason, all high-speed, long-haul optical communication systems avoid direct laser modulation entirely, using instead an external modulator (typically a Mach-Zehnder interferometer) that varies the light's amplitude without touching the laser's injection current, leaving the gain and refractive index undisturbed and keeping the output frequency stable.
\end{keyinsight}

\section*{From General Theory to Semiconductor Realities}

Throughout this chapter, we have built a rigorous macroscopic model of the laser oscillator. By introducing optical feedback via the Fabry-P\'{e}rot cavity, we successfully constructed a self-sustaining device that requires no external seed signal. We quantified the oscillation threshold, analysed the spatial modes determined by cavity mechanics, explored the mode competition driven by homogeneous versus inhomogeneous broadening, calculated the steady-state output power derived from gain saturation, and finally uncovered the subtle coupling between gain and phase that causes frequency pulling and chirping in real operating lasers.

However, a fundamental abstraction remains at the core of our oscillator model: \textbf{we have treated the gain medium as a generic collection of isolated atoms}. In reality, the most transformative and widely deployed lasers in the modern world --- from the fibre-optic backbone of the internet to the barcode scanner --- do not use isolated atoms floating in a gas. They use tightly packed crystal lattices where billions of atoms interact continuously. A generic atomic model cannot systematically explain how to inject carriers into these bands or how a simple electrical current produces optical gain.

\begin{keyinsight}[The Missing Physics: Solid-State Semiconductors]
    To transform our theoretical oscillator into the immensely practical, electrically driven devices that dominate modern optoelectronics, we must introduce \textbf{semiconductor physics}.

    If we replace the generic atomic gas with a semiconductor PN junction, the discrete energy levels blur into continuous energy bands. Instead of pumping isolated atoms with external light, we can directly inject massive populations of electrons and holes across the junction using a simple forward bias. By physically trapping these massive carrier populations in a confined region, we can achieve the necessary population inversion entirely through electrical means.
\end{keyinsight}

This conceptual leap from a generic atomic cavity to a solid-state, electrically driven diode is the ultimate transition into applied optoelectronics. The cavity mechanics we have mastered here --- threshold conditions, mode spacing, and photon lifetimes --- will now serve as the structural framework housing this new semiconductor gain medium. The next chapter will review exactly how this band-structure physics successfully powers the LED and the \textbf{semiconductor laser diode}.
