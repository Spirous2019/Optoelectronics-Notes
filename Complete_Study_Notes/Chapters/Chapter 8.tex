\section{Putting the Rate Equation to Work}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

The carrier rate equation $dN/dt = J/(ed) - N/\tau - \Gamma G S$ is more than just a bookkeeping formula — it is the master equation that governs the entire family of semiconductor light sources. By solving it under different conditions and with different assumptions about the photon field, we recover the distinct operating regimes of three fundamentally different devices: the Light-Emitting Diode (LED), the Semiconductor Optical Amplifier (SOA), and the Laser Diode (LD).

To build a clear mental roadmap, let's look at the physical differences and how to deal with the carrier ($N$) and photon ($S$) dynamics in each device:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Light-Emitting Diode (LED):} 
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Optical Feedback:} None (no cavity or mirrors).
        \item \textbf{Photon Density ($S$):} Negligible ($S \approx 0$). Stimulated emission is completely ignored.
        \item \textbf{Carrier Governing:} Carriers rise linearly with injection current, $N = \frac{J\tau}{ed}$. Total recombination is dominated entirely by spontaneous and non-radiative processes ($N/\tau$).
        \item \textbf{Key Signature:} Incoherent, broad-spectrum output ($\sim 40\text{ nm}$ bandwidth) that scales linearly with current until thermal junction heating causes rollover.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Semiconductor Optical Amplifier (SOA):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Optical Feedback:} Suppressed by anti-reflection coatings ($R \approx 0$) to ensure single-pass traveling-wave amplification.
        \item \textbf{Photon Density ($S$):} Controlled by the external input signal strength.
        \item \textbf{Carrier Governing:} In the small-signal limit, $N$ is determined by the bias current. Under high input signal power, stimulated emission depletes the carrier inversion ($N - N_{\text{tr}} = \frac{\Delta N_0}{1 + P/P_s}$), leading to gain saturation.
        \item \textbf{Key Signature:} Reversible gain compression, amplification of a broadband input signal, and polarization/phase dependence.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Laser Diode (LD):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Optical Feedback:} High, provided by cleaved facet mirrors (reflectivities $R_1, R_2$) that form a resonant Fabry-Pérot cavity.
        \item \textbf{The Threshold Concept:} The threshold is the \textbf{minimum injected electrical current} ($I_{\text{th}}$) required for the optical gain to exactly balance the total cavity losses ($\Gamma g_{\text{th}} = \alpha + \alpha_m$).
        \item \textbf{Carrier Governing:} Below threshold ($I < I_{\text{th}}$), it acts like an LED ($N \propto I$, $S \approx 0$). Above threshold ($I > I_{\text{th}}$), the carrier density is clamped at its threshold value ($N \approx N_{\text{th}}$) because any extra injected carrier is instantly consumed by stimulated recombination.
        \item \textbf{Photon Density ($S$):} Above threshold ($I > I_{\text{th}}$), all extra current injected beyond the threshold is converted entirely into coherent photons ($S \propto I - I_\text{th}$).
        \item \textbf{Key Signature:} Coherent, extremely narrow-spectrum output ($\sim\text{MHz}$ or sub-nanometer linewidth) with a sharp threshold "kink" in the P--I curve at $I = I_{\text{th}}$.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

This chapter solves each case in turn, deriving the observable quantities — output power, bandwidth, gain saturation, threshold current, and the P--I characteristic — directly from the rate equation framework.

\section{The Light-Emitting Diode (LED)}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Steady-State Output Power}

An LED operates far below the optical gain threshold — the photon density $S$ inside the device is negligibly small (no resonant cavity, no optical feedback), so the stimulated emission term $\Gamma G S$ in the carrier rate equation is completely negligible. At DC steady state ($dN/dt = 0$), the rate equation reduces to a simple balance between injection and recombination:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{J}{ed} = \frac{N}{\tau} \implies N = \frac{J\tau}{ed}
\end{equation*}

The total carrier lifetime $\tau$ combines radiative ($\tau_r$) and non-radiative ($\tau_{\text{nr}}$) lifetimes operating in parallel:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{1}{\tau} = \frac{1}{\tau_r} + \frac{1}{\tau_{\text{nr}}}
\end{equation*}

The photon generation rate per unit volume is the radiative fraction of the total recombination:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{dS}{dt}\bigg|_\text{gen} = \frac{N}{\tau_r} = \frac{\tau}{\tau_r}\cdot\frac{J}{ed} = \eta_\text{int}\,\frac{J}{ed}
\end{equation*}
where the \textbf{internal quantum efficiency} ($\eta_{\text{int}}$) represents the fraction of injected carrier pairs that recombine radiatively to emit a photon:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        \eta_\text{int} = \frac{\tau}{\tau_r} = \frac{1}{1 + \tau_r/\tau_{\text{nr}}}
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

Multiplying by the photon energy $h\nu$ and the active volume $V = A \cdot d$ (where $A$ is the device area) converts the volumetric photon rate into total internal optical power:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        P_\text{int} = \eta_\text{int}\,\frac{h\nu}{e}\,I
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

This result has a clean physical interpretation: every electron injected has a probability $\eta_\text{int} = \tau/\tau_r$ of producing a photon, and each photon carries energy $h\nu$. The internal power scales linearly with current, limited only by the internal quantum efficiency.

\subsection{External Extraction Efficiency}

The internally generated photons face a second obstacle before escaping the chip: total internal reflection. A photon hitting the semiconductor-air interface at an angle beyond the critical angle $\theta_c$ is completely reflected back inside. The critical angle follows from Snell's law:
\begin{equation*}
    \sin\theta_c = \frac{n_a}{n_s}
\end{equation*}
where $n_s$ is the semiconductor refractive index ($n_s \approx 3.5$ for GaAs) and $n_a = 1$.

To see how light escapes, consider an isotropic light source inside the semiconductor emitting power $P_0$ uniformly in all directions ($4\pi$ steradians). The power emitted per unit solid angle is $P_0/(4\pi)$. Light can only escape if it strikes the semiconductor-air interface within the escape cone defined by the critical angle $\theta_c$. The differential solid angle is $d\Omega = 2\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta$. The fraction of light emitted within this escape cone, accounting for the transmission probability $T(\theta)$, gives the external extraction efficiency:
\begin{equation*}
    \eta_\text{ext} = \frac{1}{4\pi}\int_0^{\theta_c} T(\theta)\cdot 2\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta
\end{equation*}

At normal incidence ($\theta = 0$), the reflection coefficient is given by the Fresnel reflection equation:
\begin{equation*}
    R(0) = \left(\frac{n_s - n_a}{n_s + n_a}\right)^2
\end{equation*}
By conservation of energy, the normal-incidence transmittance is $T(0) = 1 - R(0)$:
\begin{equation*}
    T(0) = 1 - \left(\frac{n_s - n_a}{n_s + n_a}\right)^2 = \frac{4n_a n_s}{(n_a + n_s)^2}
\end{equation*}
For a high refractive index semiconductor ($n_s \gg n_a$), the critical angle $\theta_c$ is very small, meaning the escape cone is narrow. Over this narrow angle, the transmittance is approximately constant, $T(\theta) \approx T(0)$. Substituting this and evaluating the solid-angle integral:
\begin{equation*}
    \eta_\text{ext} = \frac{T(0)}{2}\int_0^{\theta_c}\sin\theta\,d\theta = \frac{T(0)}{2}(1 - \cos\theta_c)
\end{equation*}
Using the small-angle Taylor expansion for $\theta_c \ll 1$, we have $1 - \cos\theta_c \approx \theta_c^2/2 \approx \sin^2\theta_c/2$. Applying Snell's law ($\sin\theta_c = n_a/n_s$):
\begin{equation*}
    1 - \cos\theta_c \approx \frac{n_a^2}{2n_s^2}
\end{equation*}
Substituting this approximation back into the efficiency equation:
\begin{equation*}
    \eta_\text{ext} \approx \frac{T(0)}{2}\cdot\frac{n_a^2}{2n_s^2} = \frac{4n_a n_s}{(n_a+n_s)^2}\cdot\frac{n_a^2}{4n_s^2} = \frac{n_a^3}{n_s(n_a+n_s)^2}
\end{equation*}
which gives the closed-form result:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        \eta_\text{ext} \approx \frac{n_a^3}{n_s(n_a + n_s)^2}
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{Figures/Chapter 8/led_external_extraction_escape_cone_geometry.jpg}
    \caption{LED External Extraction: Escape Cone Geometry}
    \label{fig:led_external_extraction_escape_cone_geometry}
\end{figure}

For a GaAs LED ($n_s = 3.5$, $n_a = 1$): $\eta_\text{ext} \approx 1.4\%$. This brutally low number explains why practical LEDs invest heavily in geometric tricks — dome-shaped epoxy encapsulants (which reduce the refractive index step and widen the escape cone), surface texturing, and chip shaping — to extract a larger fraction of the generated light.

\subsection{LED Responsivity}

Combining both efficiencies, the total output power is:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        P_\text{out} = \eta_\text{ext}\,\eta_\text{int}\,\frac{h\nu}{e}\,I = R_\text{LED}\,I
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}
where the \textbf{LED responsivity} ($R_{\text{LED}}$) defines how efficiently the LED converts electrical current into optical power. Its units are Watts per Ampere (W/A):
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        R_\text{LED} = \eta_\text{ext}\,\eta_\text{int}\,\frac{h\nu}{e} \quad [\text{W/A}]
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

\begin{keyinsight}
    Why is Responsivity such a critical parameter?
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{System Design Metric}: It collapses all complex internal physics ($\eta_\text{int}$, photon energy) and optical geometry ($\eta_\text{ext}$) into a single practical number. If a circuit requires $10\text{ mW}$ of optical power, the responsivity immediately dictates the exact required drive current.
        \item \textbf{Linearity Indicator}: Under ideal conditions, $R_\text{LED}$ is constant, meaning the device acts as a linear converter. If you double the drive current, you precisely double the output light.
    \end{itemize}
\end{keyinsight}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{Figures/Chapter 8/led_pi_temperature.jpg}
    \caption{LED P–I Characteristic at Multiple Temperatures}
    \label{fig:led_pi_temperature}
\end{figure}

Graphically, the responsivity is simply the slope of the P--I curve. At low currents, it remains constant (the straight, linear portion of the curve). However, at high currents, the curve bends downwards—this is called \textbf{thermal rolloff}. 

The physical reason for this rolloff is \textbf{Joule heating} ($I^2R$). As high currents heat up the junction, the elevated temperature strongly increases the rate of non-radiative recombination (such as Auger recombination), which causes $\eta_\text{int}$ to plummet. Consequently, the responsivity drops. This thermal rolloff is more severe at higher ambient temperatures, which is why LED datasheets always specify output power and responsivity at a specific, strictly controlled juncti\subsection{LED Emission Spectrum}

Under weak injection conditions, where the quasi-Fermi levels for electrons ($E_{fn}$) and holes ($E_{fp}$) lie within the bandgap, the spontaneous emission rate per unit frequency interval $R_{sp}(\nu)$ is given by:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        R_{sp}(\nu) = \frac{1}{\tau_r}\rho(\nu)f_c(E_a)(1-f_v(E_b))
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\tau_r$ is the radiative lifetime.
    \item $\rho(\nu)$ is the joint density of states (DOS) per unit frequency interval.
    \item $f_c(E_a)$ is the Fermi-Dirac distribution function for the conduction band state $E_a$.
    \item $f_v(E_b)$ is the Fermi-Dirac distribution function for the valence band state $E_b$.
\end{itemize}

Since $E_{fn}$ and $E_{fp}$ lie within the bandgap under weak injection, we can use the Maxwell-Boltzmann approximation for the carrier distribution functions:
\begin{equation*}
    f_c(E_a) \approx \exp\left(-\frac{E_a - E_{fn}}{k_B T}\right)
\end{equation*}
\begin{equation*}
    1 - f_v(E_b) \approx \exp\left(-\frac{E_{fp} - E_b}{k_B T}\right)
\end{equation*}
The product of these carrier probabilities is:
\begin{equation*}
    f_c(E_a)(1 - f_v(E_b)) \approx \exp\left(-\frac{E_a - E_b - (E_{fn} - E_{fp})}{k_B T}\right)
\end{equation*}
Since the photon energy is $h\nu = E_a - E_b$, this becomes:
\begin{equation*}
    f_c(E_a)(1 - f_v(E_b)) \approx \exp\left(-\frac{h\nu - \Delta E_f}{k_B T}\right)
\end{equation*}
where $\Delta E_f = E_{fn} - E_{fp}$ is the quasi-Fermi level separation.
Substituting the joint density of states on the frequency scale:
\begin{equation*}
    \rho(\nu) = \frac{2\sqrt{2} m_r^{3/2}}{\pi \hbar^2} \sqrt{h\nu - E_g}
\end{equation*}
where $m_r = (1/m_e^* + 1/m_h^*)^{-1}$ is the reduced effective mass. Multiplying these terms and absorbing constant scaling factors (relating the carrier concentration under weak injection to the band edge energy reference), we obtain the spontaneous emission spectrum equation:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        R_{sp}(\nu) = \frac{\sqrt{(2m_r)^3}}{\tau_r \pi \hbar^2} \exp\left(\frac{E_g - h\nu}{k_B T}\right)\sqrt{h\nu - E_g}
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

This spectrum has a very specific shape:
\begin{itemize}
    \item For $h\nu < E_g$, $R_{sp}(\nu) = 0$ because there are no available electronic states within the bandgap.
    \item Immediately above $E_g$, the joint DOS dominates, causing the emission to rise as $\sqrt{h\nu - E_g}$.
    \item At higher energies, the Boltzmann exponential factor $\exp\left(\frac{E_g - h\nu}{k_B T}\right)$ dominates, representing the thermal decay of highly excited carriers.
\end{itemize}

By differentiating $R_{sp}(\nu)$ with respect to frequency, we find the peak frequency where the maximum emission occurs:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        \nu_{max} = \frac{E_g}{h} + \frac{k_B T}{2h}
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}
Thus, the peak emission energy is $h\nu_{max} = E_g + \frac{1}{2}k_B T$.

The Full Width at Half Maximum (FWHM) of this spectrum in the frequency domain is given by:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        \text{FWHM}_\nu = \Delta\nu = \frac{1.8 k_B T}{h}
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

To convert this frequency bandwidth to wavelength bandwidth, we use:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        \Delta\lambda = \frac{\lambda^2}{c}\Delta\nu
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}
For a typical GaAs LED at $T = 300\text{ K}$, this results in a spectral width $\Delta\lambda \approx 40\text{ nm}$ centered around the peak wavelength $\lambda \approx 822\text{ nm}$. This relatively broad spectrum is characteristic of incoherent spontaneous emission.

\subsection{LED for Lighting Applications}

In illumination and lighting applications, the raw optical output power in Watts is not the defining performance metric. Instead, we must measure the perceived brightness to the human eye, which is characterized by the \textbf{luminous flux}. Luminous flux per watt is the key metric, measured in \textbf{lumens per Watt (lm/W)}.

The spectral luminous flux ($\Phi_\lambda$) is defined by:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        \Phi_\lambda = P(\lambda) \times 633 \times V(\lambda) \quad [\text{lm/W}]
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $P(\lambda)$ is the spectral output power of the LED at wavelength $\lambda$.
    \item $633\text{ lm/W}$ is the peak conversion factor between optical power and luminous flux.
    \item $V(\lambda)$ is the \textbf{visibility function} (or photopic luminosity function) of the human eye.
\end{itemize}

The visibility function $V(\lambda)$ accounts for the eye's varying sensitivity to different colors. It peaks at a value of unity at $\lambda = 555\text{ nm}$ (green-yellow light) and drops off towards both the ultraviolet and infrared spectral regions.

\subsection{LED Modulation}

To analyze the speed of an LED for communication applications, we start from the carrier rate equation:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{dN}{dt} = \frac{I}{eV} - \frac{N}{\tau}
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}
where $N$ is the carrier density, $I$ is the injected current, $V$ is the active volume, and $\tau$ is the overall carrier lifetime.

If we apply a sinusoidally modulated input current:
\begin{equation}
    I(t) = I_b + I_m \exp(j\omega_m t) \tag{I}
\end{equation}
where $I_b$ is the DC bias current and $I_m$ is the AC modulation current amplitude at modulation frequency $\omega_m$.
The carrier density responds with a similar sinusoidal variation:
\begin{equation}
    N(t) = N_b + N_m \exp(j\omega_m t) \tag{II}
\end{equation}
where $N_b$ is the steady-state DC carrier density and $N_m$ is the amplitude of the AC carrier density.

Substituting equations \textup{(I)} and \textup{(II)} into the rate equation yields:
\begin{equation*}
    j\omega_m N_m \exp(j\omega_m t) = \frac{I_b + I_m \exp(j\omega_m t)}{eV} - \frac{N_b + N_m \exp(j\omega_m t)}{\tau}
\end{equation*}
By separating this equation into its DC (steady-state) and AC (time-varying) components, we get:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{DC Component}:
    \begin{keyresult}
        \begin{equation}
            N_b = \frac{\tau I_b}{eV}
        \end{equation}
    \end{keyresult}
    \item \textbf{AC Component}:
    \begin{equation*}
        j\omega_m N_m = \frac{I_m}{eV} - \frac{N_m}{\tau} \implies N_m\left(1 + j\omega_m\tau\right) = \frac{\tau I_m}{eV}
    \end{equation*}
    Solving for $N_m$ gives:
    \begin{keyresult}
        \begin{equation}
            N_m = \frac{\frac{\tau I_m}{eV}}{1 + j\omega_m\tau}
        \end{equation}
    \end{keyresult}
\end{itemize}

In this expression for $N_m$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The numerator, $\frac{\tau I_m}{eV}$, represents the \textbf{low-frequency modulation coefficient} (the carrier density amplitude when the modulation frequency approaches zero).
    \item The denominator introduces a high-frequency roll-off due to the carrier lifetime $\tau$. The \textbf{electrical bandwidth} ($f_{3dB}$) is the frequency at which the amplitude of the carrier density response drops to $1/\sqrt{2}$ of its low-frequency value, corresponding to $\omega_m\tau = 1$:
    \begin{keyresult}
        \begin{equation}
            f_{3\text{dB}} = \frac{1}{2\pi\tau}
        \end{equation}
    \end{keyresult}
\end{itemize}

\section{The Semiconductor Optical Amplifier (SOA)}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Small-Signal Amplification}

An SOA is a forward-biased semiconductor heterostructure designed to amplify an incoming optical signal via stimulated emission. To prevent the device from lasing, anti-reflection (AR) coatings are deposited on the facets to suppress feedback. 

To analyze the amplification, we start from the carrier and photon rate equations. The temporal rate equations governing the carrier density $N$ and photon density $S$ are:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{dN}{dt} = \frac{J}{ed} - \frac{N}{\tau} - \Gamma B(N - N_\text{tr})S
\end{equation*}
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{dS}{dt} = \Gamma B(N - N_\text{tr})S
\end{equation*}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $J$ is the injected current density.
    \item $d$ is the active layer thickness.
    \item $\tau$ is the overall carrier lifetime.
    \item $\Gamma$ is the optical confinement factor.
    \item $B$ is the differential gain coefficient.
    \item $N_\text{tr}$ is the transparency carrier density.
\end{itemize}

In the small-signal regime, the input signal is weak, meaning the photon density $S$ is small enough that the carrier depletion via stimulated emission is negligible. Under this condition, the carrier density $N$ remains constant. Integrating the temporal rate equation for the photon density yields:
\begin{equation*}
    S(t) = S(0)\exp\!\left(\Gamma B(N - N_\text{tr})t\right)
\end{equation*}

To express this growth as a function of propagation distance $z$ along the amplifier length, we use the relation between time and distance:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{dt}{dz} = \frac{1}{v_g}
\end{equation*}
where $v_g$ is the group velocity of the mode.
Substituting this into the photon rate equation gives the spatial rate of growth:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{dS}{dz} = \Gamma B v_g(N - N_\text{tr})S
\end{equation*}

Integrating this from the input facet ($z = 0$) to a distance $z$ yields the spatial photon density:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        S(z) = S(0)\exp\!\left(\Gamma B v_g(N - N_\text{tr})z\right)
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}
The optical intensity $I$ follows the same exponential growth, since it is directly proportional to the photon density:
\begin{equation*}
    I = v_g S h\nu
\end{equation*}

\subsection{Gain Saturation}

If the input optical signal is not negligible, stimulated emission consumes carriers faster than they can be replenished by the electrical bias, causing the gain to drop. To quantify this gain saturation, we analyze the steady-state behavior by setting the temporal carrier rate equation to zero:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{J}{ed} - \frac{N}{\tau} - \Gamma B(N - N_\text{tr})S = 0
\end{equation*}
We rewrite this by separating the carrier density term relative to the transparency carrier density:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{J}{ed} - \frac{N - N_\text{tr}}{\tau} - \frac{N_\text{tr}}{\tau} - \Gamma B(N - N_\text{tr})S = 0
\end{equation*}
Rearranging and solving for the excess carrier density $N - N_\text{tr}$ yields:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        N - N_\text{tr} = \frac{\dfrac{J}{ed}\tau - N_\text{tr}}{1 + \Gamma B S \tau} = \frac{\Delta N_0}{1 + P/P_s}
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\Delta N_0 = \frac{J}{ed}\tau - N_\text{tr}$ is the unsaturated carrier density.
    \item $P$ is the optical power, related to photon density by $P = S \cdot h\nu \cdot v_g \cdot A / \Gamma$ (with $A$ being the cross-sectional area).
    \item $P_s$ is the saturation power, defined as:
    \begin{keyresult}
        \begin{equation}
            P_s = \frac{v_g h\nu A}{\Gamma B \tau}
        \end{equation}
    \end{keyresult}
\end{itemize}

Since the optical gain coefficient $g$ is proportional to the excess carrier density, $g = \Gamma B v_g(N-N_\text{tr})$, it satuarates with power according to:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        g = \frac{g_0}{1 + P/P_s}
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}
where $g_0 = \Gamma B v_g \Delta N_0$ is the unsaturated small-signal gain coefficient.

To find the spatial power evolution, we substitute the saturated gain into the propagation equation:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{dP}{dz} = g P = \frac{g_0}{1 + P/P_s} P
\end{equation*}
Separating the variables yields:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{1}{P}\left(1 + \frac{P}{P_s}\right) dP = g_0 dz
\end{equation*}
Integrating this equation from the input facet ($z = 0, P = P_\text{in}$) to the output facet ($z = L, P = P_\text{out}$) gives the implicit transcendental equation for the output power:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        \ln\!\left(\frac{P_\text{out}}{P_\text{in}}\right) + \frac{P_\text{out} - P_\text{in}}{P_s} = g_0 L
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

At very low input powers ($P_\text{out} \ll P_s$), the logarithmic term dominates, and the equation simplifies to the linear amplification regime: $P_\text{out} = P_\text{in}\exp(g_0 L)$. As the input signal grows, the linear term $(P_\text{out} - P_\text{in})/P_s$ becomes dominant, and the gain saturates. The 3\,dB gain saturation point represents the power level at which the amplifier's gain drops to half its small-signal value, as shown in the graph below.

\subsection{Noise Figure}

Because the transitions in the active region are probabilistic, spontaneous emission occurs alongside stimulated emission. This adds random incoherent photons to the amplified signal, degrading the signal-to-noise ratio. The noise figure ($F_n$) is defined as:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        F_n = \frac{(\text{SNR})_\text{in}}{(\text{SNR})_\text{out}}
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

For a semiconductor optical amplifier, the noise figure is given by:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        F_n = 2\left(\frac{N}{N - N_\text{tr}}\right)\left(\frac{G}{G - \alpha_\text{int}L}\right)
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $N$ is the carrier density.
    \item $N_\text{tr}$ is the transparency carrier density.
    \item $G$ is the single-pass amplifier gain.
    \item $\alpha_\text{int}$ represents the internal waveguide losses.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Polarization Dependence}

Consequently, the confinement factors for the transverse electric (TE) and transverse magnetic (TM) optical modes are different ($\Gamma_\text{TE} \neq \Gamma_\text{TM}$). This causes the TE mode to experience higher gain than the TM mode, as shown in the polarization gain curves below.

\subsection{Fabry-Perot vs. Traveling-Wave Amplifiers}

If the facets of the semiconductor chip have non-zero reflectivities ($R_1, R_2$), the device behaves as a resonant Fabry-Pérot (FP) cavity. This introduces a frequency-dependent gain ripple. The Fabry-Pérot amplifier gain spectrum is given by:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        G_\text{FP}(\nu) = \frac{(1-R_1)(1-R_2)G(\nu)}{\left(1 - G\sqrt{R_1 R_2}\right)^2 + 4G\sqrt{R_1 R_2}\sin^2\!\left[\pi(\nu - \nu_m)/\Delta\nu_L\right]}
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $G(\nu)$ is the single-pass gain.
    \item $\Delta\nu_L = \frac{c}{2n_s L}$ is the longitudinal mode spacing (with $n_s$ being the semiconductor refractive index).
    \item $\nu_m = \frac{mc}{2n_s L}$ is the resonant frequency of the $m$-th cavity mode.
\end{itemize}

The peak gain occurs on resonance, and the minimum gain occurs off resonance. The gain ripple ratio ($\Delta G$), representing the peak-to-trough ratio, is:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        \Delta G = \frac{G_\text{FP}^\text{max}}{G_\text{FP}^\text{min}} = \left(\frac{1 + G\sqrt{R_1 R_2}}{1 - G\sqrt{R_1 R_2}}\right)^2
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

The 3\,dB resonance bandwidth (FWHM of a resonance peak) is:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        \Delta\nu_A = \frac{2\Delta\nu_L}{\pi}\sin^{-1}\!\left( \frac{1 - G\sqrt{R_1 R_2}}{\sqrt{4 G\sqrt{R_1 R_2}}} \right)
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

For broadband, traveling-wave amplifier (TWA) operation, the gain ripple must be minimized (typically $\Delta G < 3\text{ dB}$). This requires suppressing reflection at the facets such that:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        G\sqrt{R_1 R_2} < 0.17
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

To achieve reflectivities below $0.1\%$, TWAs utilize anti-reflection coatings alongside geometric structures to prevent reflected light from coupling back into the waveguide mode:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tilted Active Region}: The active waveguide is oriented at an angle (typically $7^\circ$ to $10^\circ$) relative to the facets, directing reflections away from the guiding core.
    \item \textbf{Windowed Active Region}: The active waveguide terminates shortly before the facet, allowing the beam to expand in a passive region so that only a tiny fraction of the reflected light couples back.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Lasing Conditions: Gain and Phase}

A laser diode is an SOA placed inside a Fabry-Pérot cavity formed by cleaved semiconductor facets with reflectivities $R_1$ and $R_2$. Sustained oscillation requires the round-trip field to reproduce itself exactly in both amplitude and phase, which splits into two simultaneous conditions.

The \textbf{gain (amplitude) condition} requires the round-trip gain to exactly compensate the round-trip losses:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        \Gamma\,g_\text{th} = \alpha + \alpha_m, \qquad \alpha_m = -\frac{1}{2L}\ln(R_1 R_2)
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}
where $\alpha$ is the distributed internal loss per unit length (free-carrier absorption, scattering) and $\alpha_m$ is the effective mirror loss — the distributed loss equivalent of the power that leaks out through the facets each round trip.

The \textbf{phase condition} selects discrete longitudinal modes:
\begin{equation*}
    2\beta L = 2m\pi \implies f_m = \frac{mc}{2nL}, \qquad m = 1, 2, 3, \ldots
\end{equation*}

These two conditions are structurally identical to general laser threshold conditions — round-trip gain equals round-trip loss, and round-trip phase is an integer multiple of $2\pi$ — now expressed in terms of the semiconductor-specific parameters $\Gamma$, $g$, and the facet reflectivities.

\end{figure}

\subsection{Photon Lifetime}

The photon lifetime $\tau_{ph}$ quantifies the average time a photon survives inside the cavity before being lost to either internal absorption or mirror transmission. It follows from the round-trip loss analysis: after one round-trip time $\tau_{rt} = 2L/v_g$, the photon density is reduced by the factor $R_1 R_2 e^{-2\alpha L}$. Expanding for small fractional loss per round trip, the exponential decay rate is:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{1}{\tau_{ph}} = v_g(\alpha + \alpha_m)
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

The mirror loss $\alpha_m = -\ln(R_1 R_2)/(2L)$ contributes on equal footing with the internal loss — every photon has some probability of escaping through the facets on each round trip, and $\alpha_m$ quantifies that probability per unit length.

\subsection{Threshold Carrier Density and Threshold Current}

Using the linear gain model $g = B(N - N_\text{tr})$, the gain condition at threshold ($\Gamma g_{\text{th}} = \alpha + \alpha_m = 1/(v_g\tau_{ph})$) gives:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        N_\text{th} = N_\text{tr} + \frac{1}{\Gamma B \tau_{ph}}
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

Below threshold, the photon density is negligible and the carrier rate equation gives $N = J\tau/(ed)$. Setting $N = N_\text{th}$ determines the threshold current density:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        J_\text{th} = \frac{ed}{\tau}\,N_\text{th} = \frac{ed}{\tau}\!\left(N_\text{tr} + \frac{1}{\Gamma B \tau_{ph}}\right)
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}
Multiplying by area $A$ gives total threshold current $I_{\text{th}} = J_{\text{th}} A$.

Every term in this expression has a physical handle: making $d$ smaller reduces the threshold current (less volume to invert), making $\Gamma$ larger reduces it (better mode-gain overlap), making $\tau_{ph}$ longer reduces it (lower loss cavity), and making $B$ larger reduces it (higher differential gain from the material).

\subsection{Below Threshold: LED-Like Behaviour}

Below the threshold current $I_\text{th}$, stimulated emission is negligible. The carrier density rises linearly with current, and the device emits broad-spectrum spontaneous light just like an LED:
\begin{equation*}
    N = \frac{J\tau}{ed}, \qquad S \approx 0
\end{equation*}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{Figures/Chapter 9/carrier_density_below_threshold.jpg}
    \caption{Carrier Density Below Threshold}
    \label{fig:carrier_density_below_threshold}
\end{figure}

The device is simply an LED with a cavity around it — the cavity does nothing useful below threshold, and the output is incoherent.

\subsection{Above Threshold: Carrier Clamping and the P--I Curve}

Above threshold, stimulated emission takes over. Any additional carriers injected beyond $N_\text{th}$ are almost instantly converted into coherent photons — the gain cannot rise above the threshold value without being immediately saturated back down by the growing photon density. 

This is \textbf{carrier clamping}: the same inversion-stabilising feedback that we saw in any saturated gain medium, now playing out inside the laser diode cavity. Specifically, if the carrier density $N$ tries to exceed $N_{\text{th}}$, the gain $g$ exceeds the losses, causing the photon field $S$ to grow exponentially. This massive stimulated field instantly consumes the excess carrier pairs, pulling $N$ back down. Thus, the carrier density in the active region is pinned (clamped) at its threshold value:
\begin{equation*}
    N \approx N_\text{th} \quad (I > I_\text{th})
\end{equation*}
And the material gain is clamped at the threshold loss line: $g \approx g_{\text{th}}$.

Substituting $N = N_\text{th}$ into the steady-state photon rate equation ($dS/dt = 0$) and solving for $S$:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        S = \frac{\tau_{ph}}{ed}(J - J_\text{th}) + \beta\,\frac{\tau_{ph}}{\tau_r}\,N_\text{th}
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

The first term is the dominant above-threshold contribution — every increment of current above $J_\text{th}$ goes directly into the photon field. The second term is the small spontaneous emission seeding the lasing mode (characterised by the spontaneous emission coupling factor $\beta$), which is usually negligible in edge-emitting laser diodes where $\beta \ll 1$.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{Figures/Chapter 8/ld_dc_characteristics.jpg}
    \caption{Laser Diode DC Characteristics: $N$ and $S$ vs. Current}
    \label{fig:ld_dc_characteristics}
\end{figure}

To derive the output power through facet $R_1$, we relate it to the rate of photon generation. Above threshold, the carrier density is clamped at $N_\text{th}$, so any additional injected current above $I_\text{th}$ is converted into photons by stimulated emission. The rate of photon generation in the cavity is:
\begin{equation*}
    \left.\frac{d N_p}{dt}\right|_\text{stim} = \frac{I - I_\text{th}}{e}
\end{equation*}
The generated photons decay at rate $1/\tau_{ph} = v_g(\alpha + \alpha_m)$. The fraction of photons that escape the cavity through the mirrors (mirror loss) rather than being absorbed internally is:
\begin{equation*}
    \eta_\text{mirror} = \frac{\alpha_m}{\alpha + \alpha_m} = v_g \alpha_m \tau_{ph}
\end{equation*}
So the total rate of photons escaping the cavity from both mirrors is $\eta_\text{mirror} \frac{I - I_\text{th}}{e}$. The mirror loss coefficient $\alpha_m$ is the sum of the losses from mirror 1 and mirror 2:
\begin{equation*}
    \alpha_m = \alpha_{m1} + \alpha_{m2} = \frac{1}{2L}\ln\!\left(\frac{1}{R_1}\right) + \frac{1}{2L}\ln\!\left(\frac{1}{R_2}\right)
\end{equation*}
The fraction of escaping photons that exit specifically through mirror 1 is $F_1 = \alpha_{m1}/\alpha_m$. Multiplying the photon generation rate, the escape fraction, the mirror 1 fraction, and the photon energy $h\nu$ yields the output power:
\begin{align*}
    P_\text{out} &= h\nu \cdot F_1 \cdot \eta_\text{mirror} \cdot \left(\frac{I - I_\text{th}}{e}\right) \\
    &= h\nu \left( \frac{\frac{1}{2L}\ln(1/R_1)}{\alpha_m} \right) (v_g \alpha_m \tau_{ph}) \left(\frac{I - I_\text{th}}{e}\right) \\
    &= \left( \frac{1}{2L}\ln\!\frac{1}{R_1} \cdot \frac{h\nu v_g \tau_{ph}}{e} \right) (I - I_\text{th})
\end{align*}
Thus, the output power through facet $R_1$ is:
\begin{equation*}
    P_\text{out} \approx \eta_d\,(I - I_\text{th})
\end{equation*}
where the \textbf{slope efficiency} (or differential efficiency) is:
\begin{keyresult}
    \begin{equation}
        \eta_d = \frac{1}{2L}\ln\!\frac{1}{R_1}\cdot\frac{h\nu v_g \tau_{ph}}{e}
    \end{equation}
\end{keyresult}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{Figures/Chapter 9/ld_pi_characteristic.jpg}
    \caption{Laser Output Power vs. Drive Current}
    \label{fig:ld_pi_characteristic}
\end{figure}

This linear above-threshold P--I relationship — with its sharp kink at $I_\text{th}$ — is the defining characteristic of every laser diode. Measuring the kink and the slope gives $I_\text{th}$ and $\eta_d$ directly, the two most important DC performance parameters.

\subsection{Temperature Dependence}

Temperature degrades both the threshold current and the slope efficiency. The empirical characterisation commonly used is:
\begin{equation*}
    I_\text{th}(T) = I_\text{th}(T_\text{ref})\exp\!\left(\frac{T - T_\text{ref}}{T_0}\right)
\end{equation*}
\begin{equation*}
    \eta_d(T) = \eta_d(T_\text{ref})\exp\!\left(-\frac{T - T_\text{ref}}{T_1}\right)
\end{equation*}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{Figures/Chapter 9/ld_pi_temperature.jpg}
    \caption{Temperature Dependence of the Laser P–I Curves}
    \label{fig:ld_pi_temperature}
\end{figure}

where $T_0$ (the characteristic temperature) quantifies threshold sensitivity to temperature — a higher $T_0$ means the threshold rises more slowly with temperature, which is desirable. At a fixed drive current, the P--I curve shifts right (higher threshold) and flattens (lower slope) as temperature rises. This is why high-power laser modules require thermoelectric coolers to stabilise the junction temperature.

\section{From Static Behaviour to Dynamics}
% ------------------------------------------------------------------

The DC picture of the laser diode we have built here — threshold, carrier clamping, and the linear P--I curve — explains the static behaviour beautifully. However, a laser diode used in a communications system is never just sitting at DC. The data stream modulates the drive current at gigabit-per-second rates, and the laser must respond faithfully to every current fluctuation.

\begin{keyinsight}[The Limitation of Static Analysis]
    The DC analysis treats the coupled carrier-photon system as if it were always in steady state. This works perfectly for calculating threshold currents and slope efficiencies, but it completely misses two critical dynamic effects:

    First, the laser cannot respond instantaneously to a current step — there is a \textbf{turn-on delay} while the carrier density builds from its initial value up to the threshold. This limits how quickly the device can be switched.

    Second, after turn-on, the carrier and photon densities do not immediately settle to their new steady-state values. They overshoot, undershoot, and ring at a characteristic frequency before equilibrating. These \textbf{relaxation oscillations} limit the modulation bandwidth and introduce pattern-dependent distortion in high-speed data links.

    Both phenomena are invisible to the DC rate equation but emerge naturally from the coupled carrier-photon dynamics when we solve the full time-dependent system.
\end{keyinsight}

The next chapter will linearise the coupled rate equations around the above-threshold bias point, derive the small-signal modulation transfer function, extract the relaxation oscillation frequency and the damping factor, and then analyse the large-signal turn-on delay — completing the dynamic picture of the laser diode and connecting it to single-frequency architectures like the DFB and DBR laser.
